Valorization of wet biowaste into sustainable aviation fuel presents a promising
opportunity to decarbonize the aviation industry. While hydrothermal lique-
faction of food waste produces high energy content biocrude oil, its complex
composition introduces challenges for achieving drop-in fuel properties. This
research demonstrates a pathway from food waste-derived biocrude to sus-
tainable aviation fuel through single-stage hydrotreating using cobalt molyb-
denum catalyst. Hydrotreating parameters are screened via the Taguchi
method and optimized via Response Surface Methodology. The fuel candidate
meets American Society for Testing and Materials jet fuel properties without
blending, including low sulfur limits, density, viscosity, ﬂash point, and freeze
point. Further, Circularity Index, the fraction of renewable and recovered
resources, demonstrates that compared to conventional jet fuel, this pathway
improves energy and carbon circularity by 31.1% and 17.0%, respectively.
Additionally, this approach identiﬁes future direction, including reaction and
catalyst tuning for increased isomerization, to improve fuel properties.
Rising energy demand and the negative impacts of greenhouse gas
(GHG) emissions have hastened efforts to produce sustainable aviation
fuel (SAF). To achieve the United States’s SAF Grand Challenge of
meeting 100% domestic demand for aviation fuel and net-zero carbon
emissions, a signiﬁcant uptake in SAF is required. SAF can form a cir-
cular carbon loop that utilizes carbon emissions during production,
transportation, and combustion, effectively reducing aviation’s carbon
footprint1. Further, aviation represents 2–3% of global GHG emissions
and 11% of U.S. GHG emissions2,3. However, SAF can reduce carbon
emissions by 80% compared to traditional jet fuel3–5, largely due to the
use of biogenic waste material, which allows uptake of feedstock to
offset combustion carbon emissions from SAF3.
Currently, there are eight SAF production pathways and three co-
processing pathways approved by the American Society for Testing
and Materials (ASTM)6. Synthesized parafﬁnic kerosene from hydro-
processed esters and fatty acids (HEFA-SPK) is the most mature and
least expensive pathway, but it is severely constrained by feedstock
availability7. HEFA-SPK hydroprocesses lipid feedstocks, including
FOGs, algae, vegetable oils like soybean oil, and seed oils like jatropha.
Furthermore, synthesized parafﬁnic kerosene from hydroprocessed
hydrocarbons, esters, and fatty acids (HC-HEFA-SPK) utilizes an addi-
tional feedstock, algae oil from Botryococcus braunii, to produce SAF.
Fischer-Tropsch hydroprocessed synthesized parafﬁnic kerosene (FT-
SPK) converts woody biomass (e.g., municipal solid waste, agricultural

The utilization of waste biomass plays a signiﬁcant role in the
production of biofuels for the transportation sector and reduction of
GHG emissions. Biofuels can be deﬁned as ﬁrst-generation (edible oil
seeds), second-generation (non-edible oil seeds, waste materials),
third-generation (algae), and fourth-generation (genetically engi-
neered oil crops) according to their feedstock8. In particular, second-
generation feedstocks avoid land use and enable the recovery of car-
bon and energy from waste streams, presenting a sustainable feed-
stock for fuel production. Approximately 11% of bio-based energy and
chemicals in the U.S. are produced from waste biomass9. Potential
biomass feedstocks from waste streams include waste lipids like FOGs
(fat, oil, and grease), food waste, algal blooms, sewage sludge, and
agricultural and forestry residues. While catalytic hydroprocessing of
lipid feedstocks, such as waste cooking oils, is currently the most
competitive pathway for renewable diesel and SAF, feedstock sus-
tainability is limited by availability and cost10. One promising feedstock
for SAF is food waste, an impactful biomass stream that represents
reusable carbon sources for renewable energy applications. In the
United States, food waste is the most common discarded material for
landﬁll and incineration11. Over 30% of food is wasted or lost in the
global supply chain each year; In 2021, it was reported that 13.2% was
lost in the chain from farm to consumer, while an additional 17% was
wasted at the consumer level, including households, food service, and
retail12. Furthermore, disposal of food waste in landﬁlls has further
environmental
impacts
through
GHG
emissions
during
decomposition11. Food waste is an ideal feedstock for hydrothermal
liquefaction (HTL), a thermochemical conversion method that reacts
wet biomass under high heat and pressure to produce biocrude oil,
along with aqueous, char, and gaseous byproducts. Compared to other
thermochemical technologies, HTL has an economic advantage by
using the inherent water content of feedstocks as a reaction medium,
thereby avoiding the time-consuming and cost-prohibitive drying
pretreatment step13. Moreso, it can convert a broad range of feedstock,
including non-lipid components of biomass, such as proteins and
carbohydrates14. While HTL biocrude is a promising substitution for
liquid petroleum products such as transportation fuels, it possesses
several undesirable physicochemical properties that make it unsui-
table as a drop-in fuel, including high oxygen content; high viscosity
and acidity; and low heating value13,15. Furthermore, biocrude oil
derived from biogenic feedstocks contain alkali impurities and sulfur
that can cause catalyst deactivation over time16. Nonetheless, HTL’s
ability to convert a broad range of feedstocks gives it an advantage
over other SAF conversion technologies, such as HEFA-SPK, which is
sensitive to feedstock volatility, price, and availability7. For example,
the minimum fuel selling price (MFSP) of HEFA-SPK in the United
States ranged from $1.84–9.40/GGE, depending on feedstock source7.
Comparatively, the MFSP of SAF from HTL has been reported from
$2.77/GGE for sewage sludge feedstock17, $3.34/GGE for food waste18,
and up to $5.42/GGE for cultivated algae19. Furthermore, 8-18% of costs
were associated with the required upgrading of HTL biocrude oil,
including hydrotreating and hydrocracking17,19. For feedstocks requir-
ing deeper heteroatom removal, the addition of a hydrodenitrogena-
tion unit only added $0.02/GGE19.
Valorization of biowaste feedstocks like food waste into renew-
able transportation fuels such as SAF promotes both feedstock and
energy sustainability, as well as a zero-waste circular bioeconomy.
Furthermore, this waste-to-energy paradigm contributes to the United
Nations Sustainable Development Goals (SDG) 7, 12, and 13. In the 2023
SDG Report, it was found that although 28.2% of electricity came from
renewable energy sources, growth in clean energy for the transporta-
tion sector has been much more limited, with only 4.0% from renew-
able sources12. The aviation industry, like most of the transportation
sector, is difﬁcult to electrify, so HTL represents a potential solution to
pivot away from fossil fuels and achieve SDG 7 Affordable and Clean
Energy. Furthermore, SAF from food waste directly serves SDG 12
Responsible Consumption and Production by providing a renewable
alternative to alleviate fossil fuel consumption, as well as utilizing a
feedstock that helps the goal to decrease food waste and loss 50% by
203012. Lastly, SAF production and consumption improves carbon
circularity, therefore working toward SDG 13 Climate Action to reduce
GHG emissions and mitigate global warming12.
HTL biocrude must be upgraded to remove inorganic compo-
nents and improve its fuel properties. Depending on the feedstock,
different challenges and upgrading routes exist. For example, HTL
feedstocks with high protein content, such as algae, typically produce
nitrogenous biocrude that may require deeper heteroatom removal
with more severe or multi-stage hydrotreating20,21. Lignocellulosic oil
contains a signiﬁcant amount of aromatics desirable for SAF, but the
complex composition of lignin compounds make them recalcitrant to
hydrotreating, necessitating reaction engineering and catalyst devel-
opment for efﬁcient hydrodeoxygenation22. Furthermore, HTL of high-
lipid feedstocks typically produces heavier biocrude compounds due
to
repolymerization
reactions,
requiring
hydrocracking
during
upgrading23. Common technologies used for biocrude upgrading
include
distillation,
esteriﬁcation,
emulsiﬁcation,
hydrotreating,
hydrocracking, and steam reforming15,24,25. Catalytic hydrotreating can
target removal of heteroatoms, as well as improve hydrogen content
and fuel quality of the biocrude, while avoiding carbon loss from
decarbonylation and decarboxylation reactions20. Previous studies
have explored hydrotreating biocrude from various feedstocks
including lignocellulose, agricultural waste, algae, sewage sludge,
waste cooking oil, and food waste. Molybdenum-based catalysts are
desirable for their low cost, comparable activity to noble-metal cata-
lysts, and effectiveness toward deoxygenation (de-O). Cobalt molyb-
denum catalysts have been shown to be slightly more effective for
denitrogenation (de-N) compared to nickel molybdenum. For exam-
ple, 99% de-N was achieved for hydrotreated HTL biocrude using
CoMo26,
while
NiMo
achieved
up
to
80%
using
single-stage
hydrotreating27, and 92.5% using two-stage hydrotreating21. Addition-
ally, Cronin et al. found that CoMo led to increased removal of het-
erocyclic nitrogenates, such as pyridine and pyrimidine28. Catalysts
with higher metal loads exhibit improved yield and de-O29, and while
organic solvents such as methanol, ethanol, and 2-propanol can act as
hydrogen donors, they may also cause competing reactions between
hydrogenating the solvent and biocrude oil30.
Hydrotreating temperatures for HTL biocrude typically range
from 350 to 400 °C and pressures of 4–10 MPa20,27,28. The use of lower

Depending on the reactor conﬁguration (batch vs. continuous), the
catalyst load and retention time (RT) may be deﬁned individually or as
a weight hourly space velocity (WHSV, h−1). In batch reactors, the
equivalent WHSV can be deﬁned as the ratio of biocrude to catalyst
mass divided by the RT. For continuous hydrotreating, the 0.3–1.0 h−1
WHSV was used, with 0.5 h−1 the most common27,28,31,32. Meanwhile,
batch reactors applied catalyst:oil mass ratios of 0.5 and RTs of 4 h,
yielding an equivalent WHSV of 0.5 h−1, making them comparable to
conditions in a continuous reactor20,21,32. Existing studies showed that
temperature and pressure are signiﬁcant factors for de-O and de-N,
while also inﬂuencing fuel quality and product yields29,33. Although
higher temperature increased oil recovery and is required for further
de-N, it may also lead to polymerization, condensation, and cracking
reactions due to instability of reactive oxygenates32,34. At higher
hydrogen pressure, the extent of de-O increased, while coking and
decarboxylation reactions were minimized and there was improved
hydrogenation of heterocyclic nitrogenates20,27,30. However, at severe
pressures, polymerization of small molecules may also occur,
decreasing the ﬁnal oil yield29. Meanwhile, low pressures may result in
the diffusion of gases formed during hydrocracking reactions, causing
biocrude oil to be increasingly converted into gas and lighter
molecules29. The RT also inﬂuenced product yields and fuel quality,
with longer times allowing for further de-O and hydrocracking
reactions29,30. But at extended reaction times (>4 h), polymerization
reactions occured and increased coke formation caused catalyst
deactivation29,30.
Although previous works have also produced SAF candidates
through the catalytic treatment of food waste-derived precursors,
their resulting fuel viability was restricted by blending limits, nitrogen,
and sulfur levels. For example, Cronin et al. hydrotreated biocrude
from HTL of food waste using CoMo and NiMo, but 0.86–1.34 wt%
nitrogen and 0.02–0.03 wt% sulfur remained28. Meanwhile, catalytic
upgrading of volatile fatty acids (VFA) from the anaerobic digestion of
food waste produced a neat SAF candidate, i.e., unblended, with 26–68
ppm nitrogen, slightly above the ASTM limit of 15 ppm16. Additionally,
the VFA-SAF candidate was limited by its ﬂash point and low tem-
perature viscosity, requiring at least 70% blending with Jet A to meet
ASTM speciﬁcation limits in Tier α and β tests16. In this study, it was
shown that cobalt molybdenum catalyst effectively upgraded food
waste-derived HTL biocrude oil into drop-in SAF. Through parameter
screening and optimization, a neat SAF candidate was produced that
met preliminary ASTM qualiﬁcations for jet fuel properties, possessing
low nitrogen and sulfur levels, thereby advancing circularity of the
aviation sector.
Results and discussion
Hydrotreating parameter screening
The products yields, elemental composition, and parameter screening
results are displayed in Fig. 1. It was seen that oil yield generally
decreased with combinations of higher parameter levels (Fig. 1A). This
was mainly due to polymerization reactions occurring during exten-
ded RTs that increased char formation, as well as increased hydro-
cracking and gasiﬁcation reactions at high pressure and temperature.
Greater amounts of aqueous and char products were seen at the low
and medium temperatures, indicating that at higher severity, less
polymerization and recondensation occurs over gasiﬁcation. Lack of
aqueous product at higher temperatures suggested decarboxylation
and decarbonylation pathways over hydrodeoxygenation, yielding
gaseous products rather than water. In general, higher de-O resulted in
increased higher heating values (HHV) (Fig. 1A, B). 76–100% de-O was
achieved, with up to 2.21 wt% remaining oxygen in the oil. Hydro-
treated oil with remaining oxygen were treated at lower temperature
and RT, indicating the importance of these parameters for de-O.
Desulfurization (de-S) was effective at higher severities, with up to 94%
sulfur removal and 20 ppm remaining. De-N was least efﬁcient, ranging
from 7 to 23% with 0.55–0.66 wt% nitrogen remaining in the hydro-
treated oil.
Improved fuel quality and heteroatom removal were obtained at
the expense of carbon and energy recovery (Cr and Er), especially due
to carbon losses from gasiﬁcation at severe hydrotreating conditions.
The most signiﬁcant parameters affecting de-O, Er, and Cr were
determined using Taguchi delta values, the difference between the
highest and lowest mean of response (Fig. 1C). For de-O, the most
signiﬁcant parameters were catalyst-to-oil ratio (cat:oil) > temperature
(T) > hydrogen load-to-biocrude oxygen ratio (H:O) > retention time
(RT), while for Cr and Er they were T > cat:oil > RT > H:O. Though H:O
was more important for oxygen removal than carbon and energy
recovery, the highest average de-Os were obtained at 15:1 H:O ratios.
Therefore, T, cat:oil, and RT were selected as the parameters to
undergo optimization for SAF production while the H:O ratio was held
constant.
Improved boiling point distribution and hydrocarbon composi-
tion was also observed across changes in hydrotreating temperature
and catalyst ratio. Temperature was the most signiﬁcant factor
affecting the boiling point distribution of the hydrotreated biocrude
oil (Fig. S1A). At lower temperatures, light fractions <250 °C increased
compared to PT biocrude oil, but the amount of mid-range fractions
from 250 to 350 °C decreased while the amount of heavier fuel frac-
tions >350 °C increased. Therefore, at mild hydrotreating conditions,
polymerization and coking reactions may decrease the yield of viable
distillates in the transportation fuel range. Notably, at the highest

Hydrotreating parameter optimization and fuel properties
Average carbon number and hydrocarbon type are signiﬁcant prop-
erties in predicting the properties of SAF37,38. Having an average carbon
number closer to conventional jet fuel’s is desirable to stay within
ASTM speciﬁcation limits for SAF. Contour heat maps elucidated the
effect of T, RT, and cat:oil on the average carbon number of hydro-
treated biocrude oil (Fig. 2A–C and Table S5). It was seen that T had a
greater effect than RT on average carbon number, with decreased
carbon number correlated with increased hydrotreating T (Fig. 2A).
The slight tilt of the contour lines toward the left also reﬂected
decreased carbon number at longer RT, indicating breakdown of the
carbon chains with limited repolymerization. Similar trends were seen
for T and cat:oil, with decreased carbon number with increased T at all
levels of cat:oil (Fig. 2B). Meanwhile, the change in carbon number
across RT and cat:oil was less pronounced (Fig. 2C). A diagonal of
slightly higher average carbon numbers was observed from high cat:oil
and low RT to low cat:oil and high RT, showing that at mild combi-
nations of cat:oil and RT, less cracking occurred. The diagonal pattern
across the axis ranges was mainly attributed to the greater effect of T
on average carbon number. For example, points on the contour line
where average carbon number ~13, were those where different T (350
vs. 450 °C) were used at the same RT and cat:oil. Therefore, the change
in T caused deviations in the average carbon number. Compared to T,
RT, and cat:oil had less effect on carbon number. Overall, the average
carbon number further decreased at combinations of higher T, RT, and
cat:oil. Therefore, while a higher temperature was required to achieve
an average carbon number closer to jet fuel, consideration should be
taken in its combination with RT and cat:oil to avoid SAF products with
carbon chains that are too light.
The modiﬁed Van Krevelen diagrams exhibit effective de-O and
de-S during hydrotreating, but varying de-N (Fig. 2D–E). A majority of
the SAF samples yielded complete oxygen and sulfur removal, with O/
C and S/C molar ratios of 0. The H/C molar ratio of Jet-A and other
conventional jet fuels depend on their speciﬁc composition. In this
case, the obtained Jet-A sample was measured to have an H/C ratio of
1.93. Nearly all the hydrotreated HTL biocrude had H/C > 1.90 and
some had H/C > 2.00, reﬂecting their comparable elemental compo-
sition to Jet-A, high HHV, and potential for improved combustion. A
wide range of de-N was observed, indicating the importance of
hydrotreating parameter tuning on nitrogen removal. Considering the
cold-weld CHN method used, the variation may also be due to sys-
tematic (instrumental or measurement) errors. With a nitrogen
detection limit of 0.1%, at least 87% de-N was achieved in a few cases.

Comparing the hydrocarbon type, carbon number distribution,
and boiling point distribution of HTL biocrude oil before and after
hydrotreating, it was conﬁrmed that hydrotreating at high tempera-
ture produced SAF samples with composition closest to Jet-A (Fig. 3).
Hydrotreating successfully improved the hydrocarbon composition,
indicated by an increase in the n-alkane content of all samples (Fig. 3A).
Like the trend seen in the parameter screening tests, hydrotreating at
combinations of higher temperature, RT, and catalyst load further
improved the hydrocarbon type to contain increased amounts of
aromatics, isoalkanes, and cycloalkanes. Notably, several of the SAF
samples contained similar or higher amounts of isoalkanes and
cycloalkanes compared to Jet-A, along with lower aromatic content.
This could lead to improved cold ﬂow properties and decreased
contrails40. Notably, the Jet-A sample contained 35% aromatics and
synthesized jet fuel must have 8-25% aromatics content. All samples
hydrotreated at 450 °C (B2, B4, B6, B8) met the low level aromatics
requirement of at least 8%, ranging from 9 to 13%. As seen in our SAF
candidate, this value is also expected to increase after taking the jet cut
from the whole hydrotreated biocrude due to the higher abundance of
aromatics in the C8–C16 range. Recent work has also indicated that
cycloalkanes can replace the volume swell properties of aromatics in
jet fuel41.
HTL biocrude oil consistently became lighter after hydrotreating.
This was reﬂected in the boiling point distribution, where hydro-
treating increased the fraction of distillate ranges <250 °C (Fig. 3B).
Like what was seen during the parameter screening, hydrotreating at
the lowest parameter levels (e.g., B5) caused polymerization reactions
that formed heavier compounds with boiling points above 350 °C. On
the other hand, hydrotreating at higher temperatures and combina-
tions of increased RT and cat:oil produced samples mainly composed
of fractions with distillate ranges <250 °C. Speciﬁcally, all samples
hydrotreated at the highest temperature level had a boiling point
distribution most similar to Jet-A.
The carbon number range in Jet-A is typically C8–C16. This was
conﬁrmed in the commercial Jet-A sample tested, where 95% of its
compounds were within the C8-C16 range and its average carbon
number was 11.2 (Fig. 3C). Chemical compounds were also grouped
into <C8, C17–C20, and >C20, to represent potential distillates in the
ranges for gasoline, diesel, and heavy oils, respectively. All samples

SAF candidacy: Tier α tests for hydrotreated HTL biocrude oil
Based on the parameter screening and optimization, the HTL biocrude
was hydrotreated to obtain a SAF candidate for Tier α prescreening of
jet fuel properties that are critical for combustor operability limit. The
SAF candidate achieved a carbon and energy recovery of 85.2 and
90.2%, respectively. Complete de-O and de-N was achieved, along with
a ﬁnal sulfur content of 10 ppm, meeting the 15 ppm maximum42. The
physicochemical properties were further listed in Table S6. Compared
to conventional jet fuel, the SAF candidate from this work contained
higher amounts of light hydrocarbons, was within ASTM speciﬁcation
limits for critical bulk properties, and had a comparable distillation
range predicted within speciﬁcation limits (Fig. 4). The hydrocarbon
compositions of both the hydrotreated HTL biocrude oil and SAF cut
were mainly n-alkanes, followed by cycloalkanes, aromatics, and iso-
alkanes (Fig. 4A, B). Compared to the average jet fuel carbon dis-
tribution, the hydrotreated HTL biocrude oil contained high amounts
of lighter (<C9) and heavier (>C13) hydrocarbons (Fig. 4A). The greater
amount of heavy compounds in the whole hydrotreated HTL biocrude
oil caused the average carbon number to be 12.7, slightly higher than
jet fuel’s 11.4. However, after taking the SAF cut from C9 to C13, the
average carbon number was more similar to jet fuel, decreasing to 10.7
(Fig. 4B). This SAF cut represented 31.5 wt% of the hydrotreated HTL
biocrude oil. Meanwhile, the light and heavy ends are also potential
candidates for biobased gasoline and diesel.

It was observed that the surface tension (σ), density (ρ), kine-
matic viscosity (ν), lower heating value (LHV), ﬂash point, derived
cetane number, and freeze point were all within ASTM speciﬁcation
limits (Fig. 4C). Notably, the density and freeze point of the SAF
candidate were near, but still, within ASTM speciﬁcation limits, which
was attributed to the high amount of heavy-end n-alkanes. At the
same time, LHV was even higher than conventional jet fuel, likely due
to the higher concentration of n-alkanes, which have high speciﬁc
energy43. Although n-alkanes have high energy content and thermal
stability, light n-alkanes (<nC7) can cause ﬂash point violations and
heavy n-alkanes (>nC14) can cause freeze point violations43. Since the
SAF cut contained >50% n-alkanes, the fuel likely has a high smoke
point, preventing soot formation and enabling cleaner combustion.
However, there were few isoalkanes present in the hydrotreated HTL
biocrude oil and SAF cut. Lightly branched isoalkanes are desirable
for jet fuel because they improve cold ﬂow properties, speciﬁc
energy, thermal stability, and freeze point43–45. Isoalkanes improve
the pour point and cloud point of jet fuel, which are especially
important for the operability and performance of jet fuel at high
altitude and low temperature46,47. Meanwhile, the high content of
cycloalkanes and aromatics (~44%) also help to improve performance
and handling properties of the SAF. For instance, monocycloalkanes
improve the density, freeze point, and speciﬁc energy of the fuel,
while polycycloalkanes increase energy density, speciﬁc energy, and
thermal stability43. Aromatics are needed to promote seal swelling in
jet engines, but they have high particulate emissions and cause sig-
niﬁcant sooting during burning. Lastly, the predicted ASTM D86
distillation curve of the SAF candidate was similar to the conven-
tional jet range (Fig. 4D). Although the light and heavy ends fell
slightly below the jet range, it was still within ASTM speciﬁcation
limits. The ﬁnal boiling point for the SAF cut was set lower than the
typical range for conventional jet fuel to address concerns about the
freezing point. To further mitigate the freezing point issue, an
additional isomerization step could be implemented to convert lin-
ear alkanes into lightly branched alkanes. This adjustment would
allow the ﬁnal boiling point to be increased to the conventional jet
fuel range, resulting in a higher SAF yield. This food waste-derived
HTL-SAF candidate was predicted to meet key ASTM speciﬁcations
without blending. However, it is noted that some parameters, such as
density and freeze point, are close to the speciﬁcation limits.
Therefore, blending may be required if any variations in feedstock
composition cause deviations in the resulting fuel properties. The
mass, carbon, and energy yields for this SAF candidate (Table S7)
provided implications for scale-up and future areas of direction.
After HTL biocrude pretreatment, hydrotreating, and distillation to
obtain the SAF cut, the ﬁnal mass yield from food waste was 6.94 wt
%. For instance, improved mass transfer during hydrotreating could
increase the oil yield and decrease losses to char and gaseous pro-
ducts. As mentioned before, increased alkane branching can also
help improve the hydrotreated biocrude’s ﬁnal boiling point,
resulting in a higher SAF yield. Therefore, continuous hydrotreating,
reaction tuning, and catalyst development present potential areas of
work to improve viability of the HTL-SAF route.
Circularity index
Sustainability analysis using a circularity index (CI) demonstrated
improved circularity in the U.S. aviation industry when implementing a
HTL-SAF pathway. CI is a single value, between 0 and 1, that measures
the circularity of concern in terms of consumable, renewable, and
recoverable resources48. In this case, the energy and carbon CI was
determined for the entire existing U.S. aviation industry’s jet fuel
consumption and potential for SAF production from disposed food
waste. Average consumption and production data for petroleum
products, food waste, and the HTL process was collected for eight
value chain categories: extraction, reﬁning, distribution, consumption,
disposal, recovery, remake, and reuse (Tables S8 and S9). In the
existing U.S. aviation industry, jet fuel is mainly derived from petro-
leum products, with very little SAF consumption. In the ﬁrst half of
2024, an average of 1,657,000 barrels of jet fuel were consumed
per day49. Meanwhile, just 15.8 million gallons of SAF were consumed in
202250. In 2019, it was estimated that 66.2 million wet tons of food
waste was managed in the U.S., with 39.6 million tons going to
landﬁlls51.
After implementing the use of HTL and hydrotreating for food
waste recoverable from landﬁlls, the CIs improved signiﬁcantly. Energy
CI increased from 1.35 to 32.51% and carbon CI increased from 0.13% to
17.12%. Despite the improved circularity through producing SAF from
food waste, it is apparent that food waste alone will only be part of the
solution to provide feedstocks for SAF. Speciﬁcally, municipal solid
waste and wet waste (including food waste) is just a fraction of the
potential sustainable biomass supply for production of liquid trans-
portation fuels. Other emerging biomass feedstocks, such as algae,
energy crops, forestry, agricultural, vegetable oil, and starch supplies,
also have potential for SAF production52. Additionally, HTL biocrude
from food waste can produce other bioproducts besides SAF (such as
gasoline, diesel, and biobinders), so the potential CI of the overall
system could be much higher53. Thereby, while food waste alone can
help increase energy and carbon CI of the aviation industry by nearly
20%, utilization of other biomass feedstocks for SAF production can ﬁll
the gap to create a fully circular bioeconomy in the aviation industry
and beyond.
The pathway from food waste to SAF via HTL and hydrotreating is
complicated, involving many factors and variations. To consider the
uncertainty in CI under changes in pathway conditions, including
amount of disposed and recoverable food waste, carbon and energy
contents of food waste and SAF, and mass yields during hydrotreating
and distillation, a Monte Carlo analysis with 1,000 simulations was
conducted. A larger range of uncertainty was depicted for energy
circularity, ranging from 28.26% to 48.73% compared to 14.05–26.93%
for carbon circularity (Fig. 5A). For both CIs, higher circularity was
observed at increased food waste recovery, which was reﬂected in its
high Spearman’s rank correlation coefﬁcient (Fig. 5B). In the U.S.,
59.84% of food waste is managed by landﬁlls, with the other ~40%
managed by combustion, donation, sewer/wastewater treatment,
composting, biochemical processing, animal feed, anaerobic diges-
tion, and land application51. Therefore, the production of SAF and
value of CI for the aviation industry could continue to increase if food
waste management beyond landﬁlls was targeted for recovery in HTL
and bioreﬁnery processes. The carbon and energy content of the food
waste also had a relatively high correlation to their respective CIs,
indicating the importance of the feedstock’s biochemical composition.
Overall, increased recovery of food waste from other waste manage-
ment practices would most greatly improve the circularity of this HTL-
SAF pathway.
HTL is a promising pathway to produce SAF. Although HTL can
valorize food waste into biocrude oil, hydrotreating is still required to
remove disadvantageous heteroatoms. It was determined that tem-
perature, catalyst:oil ratio, and RT were the most impactful parameters
on hydrotreating for de-O, carbon recovery, and energy recovery.
These parameters were optimized to achieve high de-O and de-N,
producing a SAF sample that met ASTM speciﬁcation limits for several
fuel properties without blending. Future work may explore parametric
tuning and catalyst development to promote isomerization during
hydrotreating for improved cold ﬂow properties and higher SAF yield.
Furthermore, this pathway also demonstrated improved energy and
carbon circularity in the U.S. aviation sector, which could be further
increased by changing food waste management practices for higher
food waste recovery. In all, it was shown that HTL of food waste is a
potential pathway to produce drop-in SAF for a decarbonized aviation
industry.

Methods
HTL biocrude oil
Food waste from a local food processing plant in Champaign, IL was
processed in a pilot-scale continuous plug ﬂow HTL reactor at 20 wt%
solids content, 300 °C and 12.4 MPa for a 20 min RT23. Prior to
hydrotreating, multi-stage pretreating was performed to desalt the
biocrude using a water wash at 100 °C for 60 min; dewater according
to ASTM D2892 Annex X1; and deash through isolation of fraction
<350 °C via distillation39. The physicochemical characterization of the
food processing waste feedstock, initial (BC), and pretreated (PT) HTL
biocrude oil are in Table S2.
Hydrotreating experiments
Commercial catalyst CoMo/Al2O3 (ThermoFisher Scientiﬁc) was used
to hydrotreat HTL biocrude oil. First, experiments were completed in
triplicate for a Taguchi design L9 orthogonal array (Table S3) to screen
the signiﬁcance of temperature, catalyst-to-oil ratio (cat:oil w/w), RT,
and hydrogen load-to-biocrude oxygen ratio (H:O mol:mol). Then,
experiments were completed in duplicate using a Box-Behnken design
to optimize the parameter levels using response surface methodology
(Table S4). Designated amounts of biocrude oil and catalyst were
loaded into a 100 ml Series 4590 Micro Stirred Reactor (Parr Instru-
ment Company, IL, USA). The reactor was purged to remove air,
pressurized with hydrogen, then heated to desired reaction conditions
with a stir rate of 350 rpm throughout the RT. After completion of the
hydrotreating reaction, the reactor was quenched with water to cool.
The oil and aqueous phases were collected in a pre-weighed container
and separated by centrifugation. The catalyst and char products were
collected separately, rinsed with DCM, and vacuum ﬁltered. The gas-
eous phase was sampled in a gas bag (Sigma-Aldrich) for analysis. The
reactor was rinsed with DCM to recover any residual oil, char, or cat-
alyst. The DCM evaporated overnight at 60 °C and the ﬁnal oil and char
mass was recorded. The oil recovered from the solvent rinse was not
analyzed. A commercial Jet-A sample was obtained from Willard Air-
port at the University of Illinois Urbana-Champaign (Savoy, IL, USA).
