A B S T R A C T  
The production of Sustainable Aviation Fuel (SAF) utilizing used cooking oil (UCO) as a renewable bio-waste 
resource holds immense potential for promoting sustainability in the aviation sector. In this study, SAF syn­
thesis via hydrodeoxygenation of pretreated UCO over Ni-Mo/alumina-silica catalysts followed up hydro­
isomerization of deoxygenated oil has been investigated in a fixed-bed reactor. The effect of Al2O3/SiO2 ratio on 
the physico-chemical properties of Al2O3-SiO2 support and on the overall hydrodeoxygenation performance of 
the Ni-Mo catalyst supported on Al2O3-SiO2 has been investigated. NiMo catalyst supported on Al2O3-SiO2 in a 
70:30 ratio achieved 86.69 % UCO conversion during the hydrodeoxygenation step and exhibited 74.79 % jet 
range hydrocarbon fuel selectivity under mild conditions (350◦C, 1.1 h⁻1 WHSV, 6 MPa, H2/oil (v/v): 400). The 
catalyst activity remained stable for >100 hours highlighting its good stability under the experimental conditions 
investigated. This work demonstrates the potential of tailor-made supported catalysts for highly selective single- 
step conversion of UCO into jet-range hydrocarbons, offering a significant step towards the production of sus­
tainable aviation fuels.   
1. Introduction 
As per the International Energy Agency (IEA), the aviation sector 
accounted for 2 % of global energy-related CO2 emissions amounting to 
800 million tons of CO2 in 2022. At present, although the aviation sector 
accounts for a relatively small share of global emissions, it is growing 
faster than rail, road, or shipping and is one of the most challenging 
sectors to decarbonise. The environmental impact of petroleum-based 
jet fuels, primarily due to the significant emission of greenhouse gases 
(GHG), has prompted a critical examination of alternative, sustainable 
energy sources for aviation. The petroleum-based aviation turbine fuel 
(ATF) not only contribute to GHG emissions but also generate pollutants 
such as NOx, SOx, CO, particulate matter, and volatile organic com­
pounds [1]. On the other hand, aviation fuel production from sustain­
able sources such as biomass would not only provide the solution by 
reducing dependency on fossil fuels but also could offer a promising 
alternative that minimizes GHG emission. The escalating global con­
sumption of aviation fuel, predicted to increase by nearly 38 % from 
2008 levels to 2025 [2], underscores the urgency to explore sustainable 
alternatives. Biomass-derived aviation fuel has the potential to 
substitute approximately 30 % of total aviation fuel, presenting a 
long-term solution to address the aviation industry challenges [3]. In 
addition, bio-jet fuel has the potential to reduce GHG emissions by 
65–85 % compared to petroleum-based jet fuel, which will support 
mitigating environmental impact from the aviation sector [4]. Various 
pathways, including the catalytic Sugar-To-Jet (STJ), gasification fol­
lowed by Fischer-Tropsch Synthesis (FTS), Alcohol-To-Jet (ATJ), and 
Hydroprocessed Renewable Jet (HRJ) have been explored for 
manufacturing sustainable aviation fuel [5–8]. Among these routes, HRJ 
stands out as a suitable method for converting UCO and/or non-edible 
oil or animal fat-based feedstocks into aviation fuel. This process in­
volves hydrodeoxygenation and hydrocracking of triglycerides in UCO, 
transforming them into straight-chain alkanes followed by catalytic 
hydrocracking and hydroisomerization in the subsequent step to meet 
the required standards for jet fuel. Achieving desirable cold flow prop­
erties while retaining the fuel’s flash point necessitates the composition 
of straight-chain alkanes and isomer alkanes within the carbon number 
range of 9–15.

fuel encounter a plethora of hurdles, primarily stemming from the

conditions necessary due to the diverse nature and properties of vege­
table oils [9,10]. However, amidst these challenges, UCO emerges as a 
promising solution. Enriched with free fatty acids, UCO stands as a 
cost-effective and environmentally sound alternative. Widely available 
across the globe, UCO presents itself as a readily accessible resource, 
often more economically feasible than pure vegetable oils. Leveraging 
UCO not only holds the potential to substantially decrease

the environmental repercussions associated with the disposal of spent

deoxygenation of the UCO followed by hydrocracking and hydro­
isomerization of the generated paraffins involves a complex process of 
saturation, heteroatom removal, cracking, and isomerization, requiring 
bi-functional catalysts. The sulfided NiMo, known for its high hydro­
genation and hydrodesulfurization activity, is commonly employed in 
the hydrotreatment of refinery sulfur-containing feed to produce low 
and/or nil sulfur products such as naphtha, middle distillate viz kero­
sene, diesel, etc [12–15]. Furthermore, the choice of support material is 
very important for any process that involves supported catalysts. A 
carrier is usually a high-surface area inorganic material containing a 
complex pore structure through which catalytic materials are deposited. 
Earlier it was thought that support only provides a surface to disperse 
the catalytic substance to maximize the catalytic surface area. However, 
it is now quite clear that support can and often does play a critical role in 
maintaining the activity, selectivity, and durability of the finished 
catalyst. By far the most common carriers used in heterogeneous cata­
lysts are non-reducible inorganic oxides with a high surface area such as 
Al2O3, SiO2, TiO2, Al2O3-SiO2, ZrO2 etc. Al2O3 and SiO2owing to their 
excellent thermal and hydrothermal stability, low cost, and availability 
in desired purity with a well-controlled range of acidity are frequently 
employed as catalyst support. To facilitate easy access of bulk molecules 
to the active sites located within the pores, the support must exhibit a 
high surface area and large pore size. By changing the Al2O3/SiO2 ratios, 
Al2O3-SiO2 supports can be designed to meet the above objectives. 
Despite the advancements in the hydroprocessing of UCO, there is a 
gap in understanding the activity and selectivity of sulfided catalysts 
towards the production of jet-range (C9-C15) hydrocarbons through 
complex hydroconversion process chemistry. The previous studies have 
explored the catalytic hydroprocessing of UCO, revealing insights into 
conversion rates and product yields [16–24]. However, efficient cata­
lysts with extended lifetime and stability, especially for single-step HRJ 
production from UCO, remain underexplored. In addressing these gaps, 
the present study focuses on the hydroconversion of UCO over 
Ni-Mo/Al2O3-SiO2 catalysts in a fixed-bed reactor. Phosphorus is used as 
the second promoter in UCO hydroconversion catalyst. Phosphorus, a 
non-metallic element often overlooked in catalyst studies compared to 
metallic additives, plays a significant role in the NiMo/Al2O3-SiO2 
catalyst system. Research has shown that phosphorus can stabilize the 
structure of Al2O3 and prevent the phase transition of γ-Al2O3 to α-Al2O3 
at high temperatures [25]. When incorporated, phosphorus decorates 
the hydroxyl groups on the alumina surface, introducing P-OH groups 
[26] that alter the distribution of Lewis acid and Brønsted acid sites on 
the Al2O3 surface. This modification can have a profound impact on the 
catalytic properties and performance of the NiMo/Al2O3-SiO2 catalyst, 
influencing its activity and selectivity in the hydrodeoxygenation pro­
cess for converting feedstocks like used cooking oil into sustainable 
aviation fuel range hydrocarbons. The presence of phosphorus leads to 
an increase in the catalytic activity and reduction of deactivation [27]. 
The primary objective of the present study is to investigate the catalyst’s 
suitability in producing jet-range hydrocarbon molecules viz. paraffins, 
cycloparaffins, low aromatics with high yield, aligning with jet fuel 
specifications. This study not only contributes valuable information for 
optimizing hydrodeoxygenation catalyst formulation and hydro­
conversion process conditions but also introduces suitable hydro­
processing routes for producing Jet A-1 range aviation fuel from UCO 
ensuring longer lifetime and stability of catalyst.

2.2. Catalysts Preparation for Hydrodeoxygenation of used cooking oil 
The preparation of the catalyst for hydrodeoxygenation involves a 
two-stage process. The first stage focuses on synthesizing a porous 
support material. This support is composed of alumina (Al2O3) and silica 
(SiO2) with tunable Al/Si ratios. The sol-gel method is employed for this 
purpose, involving three distinct steps. and the second stage involves 
depositing nickel (Ni) and molybdenum (Mo) onto the prepared support 
to get the final catalyst. 
In the first stage, Al2O3-SiO2 support synthesis process involved three 
steps: (1) the synthesis of mixed sol; (2) the synthesis of wet gel and the 
process of aging treatment; (3) Supercritical ethanol drying. In step 1, 
alumina sol and silica sol were prepared separately by stirring the 
mixture of AlCl3⋅6 H2O/EtOH/ H2O = with the mole ratio of 1/12/35 
and TEOS/H2O/EtOH/HCl = with the mole ratio of 1/4/6/7.5×10−3 
respectively. Then both the sols were mixed in molar ratios of Al/Si = 1/ 
0, 2/1, 3/1 at 30◦C for 60 min to complete the hydrolysis. In step 2, 1,4- 
dioxane was added to the mixed sol to promote condensation. This was 
done by quickly stirring the mixture of sols at 30◦C for 15 min and sealed 
at room temperature to form the wet gel. In the process of aging treat­
ment, the wet gel was soaked in anhydrous ethanol at 50◦C to strengthen 
the network and swap out the redundant water and organic matter. 
In step 3, the wet gel was placed in an autoclave which was sealed 
and filled with anhydrous ethanol. To ensure safety requirements, the 
autoclave must be recharged with pure nitrogen gas to exhaust the ox­
ygen. The absolute pressure was controlled in the range of 0.1–9.1 MPa 
with the temperature increased to 270 ◦C at a rate of 2 ◦C/min. After the 
heat-preservation stage, the absolute pressure was slowly reduced to 
0.1 MPa by the slow release of the ethanol. Finally, the samples were 
obtained at room temperature. The serial numbers of samples, 90Al- 
10Si, and 70Al-30Si, corresponded to different mole ratios of Al/Si. 
Al2O3–SiO2 aerogel, samples were calcined at 550 ◦C respectively for 5 h 
with a rate of 3 ◦C/min. 
In the second stage for the preparation of the hydrodeoxygenation 
catalyst, the support γ-alumina (Trilobe extrudates) was first dried at 
150◦C for 2 hours and calcined at 550◦C for 5 hours to remove moisture. 
The pore volume was measured using the water-wetting technique. The 
Trilobe extrudates were then impregnated with a 0.5 wt% phosphorus 
solution (85 % H3PO4) using the incipient wetness method. This solution 
was sprayed onto the extrudates and air-dried for 12 hours at room 
temperature, followed by drying at 120◦C for 12 hours in an oven, and 
then calcined at 550◦C for 5 hours to produce a phosphorus- 
impregnated trilobe catalyst. Subsequently, 10 wt% molybdenum was

2.3. Preparation of hydroisomerization catalysts 
The preparation of hydroisomerization catalysts involves two main 
stages, the synthesis of ZSM-22 zeolite framework and the subsequent 
incorporation of platinum on template-free ZSM-22 zeolite. 
ZSM-22 samples (bulk molar SiAl = 45) were synthesized using 1,6- 
diaminohexane, NH2(CH2)6NH2 (Alfa Acer, 99 %), as a structure 
directing agent using the method reported elsewhere [28]. As per the 
method, the crystallization of ZSM-22 was performed using gel molar 
composition of 27NH2(CH2)6NH2/12 K2O/Al2O3/60SiO2/3670 H2O by 
employing potassium hydroxide, KOH (Merck, India); aluminum sulfate, 
Al2(SO4)3⋅18 H2O (Merck, India); and colloidal silica solution (LUDOX 
AS-40, Sigma-Aldrich) as precursors. Hydrothermal crystallization of 
the prepared gel was performed at 160◦C under stirred conditions for 
18 hours. The crystallized samples were then filtered, washed multiple 
times with deionized water, dried at 110◦C for 24 hours, and finally 
calcined at 550◦C for 12 hours in the presence of air to obtain the 
template-free ZSM-22 zeolite. The template-free zeolite was first 
ion-exchanged to its ammonium form using a 1 M ammonium nitrate 
solution under reflux conditions for 24 hours. The H-form of the zeolite 
was then obtained by calcining the NH4
+-zeolite at 600◦C for 6 hours 
under an airflow. The zeolite samples were subsequently loaded with 
0.5 wt% platinum using tetramine platinum (II) nitrate through an 
ion-exchange route, maintaining the solution pH around 10. The 
Pt-loaded zeolite sample (65 wt%) was then mixed with 35 wt% 
pseudo-boehmite and extruded into cylindrical extrudates of 1.6 mm 
diameter and approximately 3 mm length. These extrudates were 
calcined at 400◦C under a continuous flow of pure oxygen for 5 hours. 
The resulting Pt/H-ZSM-22 hydroisomerization catalyst had a bulk 
molar Si/Al ratio of 45. 
2.4. Catalyst characterization techniques 
The supports and catalysts were characterized by different tech­
niques. The nitrogen (N2) adsorption-desorption profiles of catalysts 
were measured using a Micromeritics ASAP-2020 plus surface area and 
porosity analyzer to derive the Brunauer–Emmett–Teller (BET) surface 
area (SBET), while the total pore volume (Vp) and average pore diam­
eter were evaluated using the Barrett–Joyner–Halenda method. The X- 
ray diffraction (XRD) patterns were recorded on a Panalytical AERIS X- 
ray diffractometer with Cu Kα emission (λ = 1.5406 Å) operating at 
40 kV and 40 mA. The framework acidity of all the samples was esti­
mated using ammonia temperature-programmed desorption (TPD) 
technique (Micromeritics Autochem Chemisorption analyzer). Typi­
cally, the samples were activated under helium flow at 300 ◦C for 1 h, 
followed by adsorption of ammonia at 100 ◦C using a gas mixture of 6 % 
ammonia in helium at a constant flow rate of 25 mL/min. A higher 
adsorption temperature was selected to minimize the extent of physical 
adsorption. Furthermore, to ensure the removal of any physically 
adsorbed ammonia, the sample was purged at 150 ◦C for 2 h under a 
flow rate of 50 mL/min of high-purity helium (99.99 %). TPD of 
ammonia was performed by heating the sample in the temperature 
range 150–900 ◦C, at a rate of 10 ◦C/min and under a helium flow rate of 
25 mL/min. The amount of ammonia desorbed from the sample was 
determined using a thermal conductivity detector (TCD) and was used to 
quantify acidity in terms of micromoles of ammonia desorbed per gram 
of zeolite sample. 
2.5. Pretreatment of the used cooking oil (UCOs) 
Used cooking oil (UCO) underwent a multi-step purification process 
to remove impurities and Free Fatty Acids (FFAs). The initial step 
involved filtering the untreated Used Cooking Oil (UCO) to eliminate 
waste residue, followed by centrifugation to separate the oil layer using 
a washing technique (hot water extraction method). Subsequently, the 
oil was aerated to induce bubbling in a separation funnel, facilitating the 
separation of FFAs from the oil layer. The resulting water/oil mixture 
underwent phase separation, yielding three distinct phases (water, FFAs, 
and oils). 
Finally, the purified oil layer, devoid of FFAs, was isolated and 
subjected to evaporation to obtain the pretreated UCO, referred to 
simply as UCO for the subsequent hydrodeoxygenation reactions. This 
meticulous pretreatment process ensures the removal of impurities and 
prepares the UCO for efficient utilization in further chemical 
transformations.

However, depending on reactor length and internal diameter, neat iso- 
octane flow can be adjusted to ensure complete catalyst wetting in the 
hydro-deoxygenation reactor.

measured as per ASTM D445 & D97 respectively. SIMDIST analysis was 
performed on liquid products to ascertain the different ranges of hy­

2.6.2. Hydroisomerization of the hydrodeoxygenated oil 
This was performed using a fixed-bed, up-flow reactor of dimensions 
47 cm × 1.1 cm (internal diameter) with a catalyst bed length of 8 cm 
and equipped with a thermowell (diameter = 3 mm, length = 40 mm) 
for measurement of the catalyst bed temperature. The catalyst was 
placed in the reactor to have a minimum temperature gradient and to 
have a sufficient layer of inert material toward the input to ensure 
proper preheating and distribution of feed before it first reached the 
catalyst zone. During the loading process, the catalyst was typically 
divided into multiple batches. Following each batch of the catalyst, 
80−120 mesh sand was packed into the void spaces of the catalyst bed, 
thus ensuring the uniformity of gas/liquid flow distribution. Typically, 
about 5 g of catalyst extrudates were loaded in the reactor as per the 
procedure. The catalyst was dried under nitrogen flow at 100 mL/min 
for 12 h at a temperature of 120 ◦C followed by reduction for a period of 
5 h at 300 ◦C. The system pressure and temperature were brought to the 
desired operating conditions, and the HDO feed was pumped at the rate 
of 7.2 mL/h in the reactor using a high-performance liquid chroma­
tography pump (HPLC pump). The product from the reactor outlet was 
cooled using a heat exchanger connected to a chiller, and it was further 
separated into gas and liquid phases in a separator. Liquid products were 
collected and subjected to simulated distillation (SIMDIST) analysis. 
Further D-86 analysis was carried out on the liquid products for 
obtaining the hydroisomerised Kero fraction (150–300 ◦C). This pour- 
point of this fraction was measured following the ASTM D97 method. 
3. Results and discussion 
3.1. Catalyst characterisation 
3.1.1. X-ray diffraction 
The acquired X-ray diffraction (XRD) patterns covered an angular 
range of 2θ = 10–80◦. The patterns were recorded with a step size of 
0.01◦and a step acquisition time of 0.5 s. The average crystallite size, L, 
was calculated using the Scherrer Eq. (1).  
L= Kλ/βcos(θ)                                                                               (1) 
where K is a dimensionless shape factor, with a value close to unity; λ is 
the wavelength of the X-ray radiation; β is the line broadening at half the 
maximum intensity (FWHM), and θ is the Bragg angle. Instrument 
broadening was considered. 
X-ray diffraction (XRD) analysis provided insights into the crystal­
linity and distribution of NiMo catalysts supported on various materials. 
Despite γ-Al2O3 predominant amorphous nature and well-dispersed 
active species, subtle distinctions became apparent with increasing sil­
ica content. The catalyst with Al2O3/SiO2 ratio of 90:10 showed faint 
signals suggesting residual silica, while the catalyst with Al2O3/SiO2 
ratio of 70:30 catalyst exhibited a broad hump indicative of amorphous 
silica. Crystalline phases of MoO3, NiO, and their interaction product 
(NiMoO4) remained elusive, particularly in the high-silica catalyst. The 
crystallite size as estimated for Ni from the Scherrer Eq. (1) is 4 nm 
possibly existing as nanoscale crystallites (sub-4 nm) or well-dispersed 
species on the high surface area supports. No NiMoO4 peaks suggested 
a potential low abundance or potential amorphous state. 
The peaks observed around 2θ values of approximately 37.5◦, 45.9◦, 
and 67.5◦; corresponding to the (311), (400), and (440) crystallographic 
planes, respectively; confirm the gamma alumina. The peak around 21◦
corresponds to the SiO2 (101) inclusion in 90:10 and 70:30 Alumina: 
Silica supports. The incorporation of more silica has increased the 
crystallinity of the support as reflected in 70:30 Alumina: Silica support. 
There are no evident peaks of Ni and Mo in the XRD pattern of the 
catalyst indicating higher dispersion and a high amorphous nature of the 
support.

Gamma alumina (γ-Al2O3) is a widely used catalyst material known 
for its acidic properties. The acidity of SiO₂-Al₂O₃ catalysts arises from 
the presence of both Brønsted and Lewis acid sites [30,32]. Brønsted acid 
sites are typically associated with the presence of hydroxyl groups (–OH) 
on the surface, which can donate protons (H⁺). These sites are often 
formed through the interaction of SiO₂ with Al₂O₃, creating Si–OH–Al 
linkages that exhibit Brønsted acidity [31]. On the other hand, Lewis 
acid sites are associated with coordinatively unsaturated aluminum 
atoms that can accept electron pairs [31–33]. Silica (SiO2) in the γ-Al2O3 
matrix significantly impacts the overall acidity of the resulting com­
posite material [31]. 
Several factors contribute to the increased acidity observed in silica- 
alumina (SiO₂-Al₂O₃) compared to pure alumina (Al₂O₃). This enhance­
ment can be attributed to the synergistic interaction between silica and 
alumina, as demonstrated by Baiker and Huang groups [34]. Bokhoven 
et al. [35] explored the correlation between Lewis acid activity and the 
presence of extra-framework aluminum species in zeolite Y. Their work 
suggests that incorporating silica into the alumina framework increases 
the dispersion of aluminum atoms. This dispersion creates more exposed 
aluminum sites, which act as Lewis acid centers. Furthermore, the for­
mation of Si–OH–Al bridges introduces new Brønsted acid sites [36]. 
Notestein et al. [37] observed the unexpected formation of these 
Brønsted acid sites upon adding SiO₂ to alumina. Additionally, silica acts 
as a structural promoter, altering the distribution and strength of acid 
sites on the Al₂O₃ support [35–37]. These mechanisms are well docu­
mented in the literature. 
The observed trends in acidity for γ-Al2O3 and its composites with 
varying SiO2 content (Table 2) show a fascinating trend of how the 
material’s composition influences its acidic properties. As expected from 
its inherent structure, pure γ-Al2O3 exhibits the presence of both weak 
and strong acid sites. This reflects the coexistence of Lewis and Brønsted 
acidity on its surface. When a small amount of SiO2 (10 %) is introduced, 
there’s a noteworthy increase in weak acid sites observed in γ-Al2O3: 
SiO2 (90:10) catalyst composition. This phenomenon can likely be 
attributed to the formation of new Brønsted acid sites at the interface 
between the Al2O3 and SiO2 phases, as discussed earlier. Interestingly, 
the strong acid sites remain relatively unchanged. This suggests that the 
10 % SiO2 content might cause a slight decrease in the concentration of 
Al3⁺ cations (which are responsible for Lewis acidity), but it’s not 
enough to significantly affect the inherent strong acid character of 
γ-Al2O3. 
When the SiO2 content is increased to 30 % (γ-Al2O3:SiO2 (70:30)) a 
sharp increase in weak acid sites is observed, likely due to a more 
prominent formation of new Brønsted acid sites at the interface 
compared to the 90:10 ratio. However, the higher concentration of SiO2 
leads to a more pronounced decrease in the number of accessible Al3⁺ 
sites. This decrease in accessible sites, coupled with the potential 
modification of existing sites by SiO2, results in a lower overall acidity 
compared to pure γ-Al2O3 and the 90:10 mixture [31]. 
The incorporation of NiMo into these catalysts further influences 
their overall acidity. While NiMo itself might not possess significant 
inherent acidity, its interaction with the acidic support plays a crucial 
role. The observed changes in acidity upon NiMo incorporation likely 
depend on the interplay between the acidic support (whether pure 
γ-Al2O3 or Al2O3-SiO2 composite) and the deposited NiMo species. This 
interplay can affect the distribution and strength of acid sites on the 
catalyst surface, potentially leading to further modifications in overall 
acidity.

The structure-activity relationship (SAR) in catalysis is a crucial 
aspect of understanding the performance of a catalyst in a given reac­
tion. It refers to the correlation between the structural features of a 
catalyst and its activity, selectivity, and stability. 
In the context of the study on highly selective hydrodeoxygenation 
for sustainable aviation fuel production from used cooking oil, the SAR 
plays a significant role in optimizing the catalyst activity and selectivity 
for converting used cooking oil (UCO) into jet-range hydrocarbons. Our 
study demonstrated the structures of pure alumina, different ratios of 
alumina-to-silica in the catalyst support play a crucial role in influencing 
the interactions and activity of converting UCO into jet-range 
hydrocarbons.  
i. Pure Alumina Structure: Pure alumina exhibits specific textural 
properties, such as a surface area of 250.14 m2/g and a pore 
volume of 0.60 cm3/g with a narrow average pore diameter of 
52.85 Å. This structure indicates a more confined network with 
smaller pores, influencing the catalyst’s surface area and pore 
characteristics.  
ii. Different Ratios of Alumina-Silica: Varying the alumina-to-silica 
ratios in the catalyst support impacts the textural properties. 
For instance, the Al2O3-SiO2 (90:10) support shows a surface area 
of 224.30 m2/g and a larger pore volume compared to pure 
alumina. As the silica content increases, the pore volume and 
average pore diameter also increase, leading to structures with 
larger pores and volumes.  
iii. Alumina-to-Silica Ratios in the Catalyst Support: The study 
identifies a 70:30 alumina-to-silica ratio as optimal for achieving 
exceptional UCO conversion (86.69 %) and jet range hydrocar­
bon fuel selectivity (74.79 %). This specific ratio influences the 
catalyst’s activity and selectivity, highlighting the importance of 
the silica-to-alumina ratio in determining the performance of the 
catalyst in converting UCO into jet-range hydrocarbons. 
The structures of pure alumina and different ratios of alumina-silica 
in the catalyst support significantly impact the textural properties, sur­
face area, pore volume, and pore diameter of the catalysts. These 
structural variations play a crucial role in determining the interactions 
and activity of the catalyst in converting UCO into jet-range hydrocar­
bons, ultimately influencing the efficiency and selectivity of the 
hydrodeoxygenation process for sustainable aviation fuel production.

Table 4 presents a summary of various catalysts and their corre­

catalyst for converting UCO into SAF-range hydrocarbons. These find­

catalyst for transforming UCO into SAF range hydrocarbons but also 
emphasize the importance of selecting appropriate catalysts and process 
parameters, such as WHSV (Weight Hourly Space Velocity), to optimize 
conversion efficiency and target the desired C9-C15 product range for 
sustainable aviation fuels. 
Origin of Catalytic Active Sites: The catalytic activity of NiMo- 
supported catalysts on Al₂O₃ and SiO₂-Al₂O₃ supports is primarily due 
to the presence of both metal active sites and acidic sites.  
(i) Metal active sites: The Ni and Mo atoms on the catalyst surface 
form active sites essential for hydrogenation and hydro­
deoxygenation reactions [47]. These sites are created during the 
impregnation and calcination processes. These metal active sites 
facilitate the activation of hydrogen and its subsequent reaction 
with unsaturated hydrocarbons, leading to the production of 
hydrocarbon biofuels.  
(ii) Acidic sites: Brønsted acid sites formed by Si–OH–Al linkages in 
SiO₂-Al₂O₃ supports [48], providing protons necessary for 
cracking and isomerization reactions. Lewis acid sites associated 
with coordinatively unsaturated aluminum atoms, these sites are 
crucial for the adsorption and activation of reactant molecules.  
(iii) Support effect: The incorporation of SiO₂ into Al₂O₃ modifies the 
support’s acidic properties and enhances the dispersion of NiMo 
particles [49,50]. This leads to a higher concentration of active 
sites and stronger acid sites in SiO₂-Al₂O₃ supports. The better 
dispersion of NiMo particles on SiO₂-Al₂O₃ supports increases the 
number of accessible active sites, contributing to the observed 
catalytic performance. 
Despite the marginal differences in catalytic results, the slight per­
formance enhancement observed for NiMo/SiO₂-Al₂O₃ catalysts can be 
attributed to improved dispersion of NiMo particles, increased number 
and strength of acidic sites, enhanced metal-support interactions. These 
factors collectively contribute to the overall catalytic performance, 
explaining the slight differences observed in Tables 4 and 5. 
3.2.3. Catalyst Stability studies 
All three catalysts were subjected to long-term testing to check the 
stability of the catalysts. The data is plotted in Fig. 6. The figure shows 
that all three catalysts show a stable trend of activity to UCO conversion 
as well as kerosene selectivity for up to 120 hours of the run without the 
requirement of catalyst re-sulfidation. This shows that the catalysts B&C 
prepared with silica-alumina supports are stable for hydro- 
deoxygenation to produce kerosene selective fraction during the HDO 
of UCO. All the catalysts were prepared by loading phosphorus on the 
support followed by impregnation of molybdenum and nickel. Phos­
phorus is considered the second promoter of hydrotreating catalysts. 
The phosphorus increases the catalytic activity and reduces the deacti­
vation of catalysts by preventing the formation of inactive NiAl2O4 
particles. It also improves the thermal stability of alumina and prevents 
coke formation during hydrotreating reaction thus ensuring enhanced 
catalyst life [25–27]. 
3.3. Hydroisomerisation of Hydrodeoxygenated Oil (HDO) 
Typically, hydroisomerization catalysts are bifunctional in nature 
and use of a noble metal such as platinum (Pt) for the dehydrogenation/ 
hydrogenation function over acidic support such as medium pore zeo­
lites like ZSM-22, ZSM-23, and ZSM-48 are reported in the literature. 
These catalysts with dual functionality are used to perform isomeriza­
tion reaction of the olefinic intermediates formed over the metal sites 
followed by branching over the acidic sites of the zeolite support during 
hydroisomerization of long chain n-paraffin. The dried HDO obtained 
from the optimized catalyst formulation CAT C (NiMo/ Al2O3-SiO2 
(70:30) was subjected to hydroisomerization and mild cracking for the 
production of jet fuel. The results of the production of Jet-Fuel from 
Hydrodeoxygenated Oil (HDO) are shown in Table 6. The conversion 
was carried over one-dimensional zeolite Pt/H-ZSM-22 based on our 
previous work on hydroisomerization of n-hexadecane at a temperature 
range of 310–330 ◦C based on our previous work on hydroisomerization 
of n-hexadecane [28]. A pour point of −60 ◦C was obtained for the 
product after the hydroisomerization and mild cracking of HDO at a 
temperature of 325 ◦C. Therefore, the (NiMo/ Al2O3-SiO2 (70:30)) 
catalyst produces a feed with the required specification to obtain jet 
range product in the isomerization reaction.
