As per the International Energy Agency (IEA), the aviation sector 
accounted for 2 % of global energy-related CO2 emissions amounting to 
800 million tons of CO2 in 2022. At present, although the aviation sector 
accounts for a relatively small share of global emissions, it is growing 
faster than rail, road, or shipping and is one of the most challenging 
sectors to decarbonise. The environmental impact of petroleum-based 
jet fuels, primarily due to the significant emission of greenhouse gases 
(GHG), has prompted a critical examination of alternative, sustainable 
energy sources for aviation. The petroleum-based aviation turbine fuel 
(ATF) not only contribute to GHG emissions but also generate pollutants 
such as NOx, SOx, CO, particulate matter, and volatile organic com­
pounds [1]. On the other hand, aviation fuel production from sustain­
able sources such as biomass would not only provide the solution by 
reducing dependency on fossil fuels but also could offer a promising 
alternative that minimizes GHG emission. The escalating global con­
sumption of aviation fuel, predicted to increase by nearly 38 % from 
2008 levels to 2025 [2], underscores the urgency to explore sustainable 
alternatives. Biomass-derived aviation fuel has the potential to 
substitute approximately 30 % of total aviation fuel, presenting a 
long-term solution to address the aviation industry challenges [3]. In 
addition, bio-jet fuel has the potential to reduce GHG emissions by 
65–85 % compared to petroleum-based jet fuel, which will support 
mitigating environmental impact from the aviation sector [4]. Various 
pathways, including the catalytic Sugar-To-Jet (STJ), gasification fol­
lowed by Fischer-Tropsch Synthesis (FTS), Alcohol-To-Jet (ATJ), and 
Hydroprocessed Renewable Jet (HRJ) have been explored for 
manufacturing sustainable aviation fuel [5–8]. Among these routes, HRJ 
stands out as a suitable method for converting UCO and/or non-edible 
oil or animal fat-based feedstocks into aviation fuel. This process in­
volves hydrodeoxygenation and hydrocracking of triglycerides in UCO, 
transforming them into straight-chain alkanes followed by catalytic 
hydrocracking and hydroisomerization in the subsequent step to meet 
the required standards for jet fuel. Achieving desirable cold flow prop­
erties while retaining the fuel’s flash point necessitates the composition 
of straight-chain alkanes and isomer alkanes within the carbon number 
range of 9–15.

fuel encounter a plethora of hurdles, primarily stemming from the

deoxygenation of the UCO followed by hydrocracking and hydro­

bi-functional catalysts. The sulfided NiMo, known for its high hydro­
genation and hydrodesulfurization activity, is commonly employed in 
the hydrotreatment of refinery sulfur-containing feed to produce low 
and/or nil sulfur products such as naphtha, middle distillate viz kero­
sene, diesel, etc [12–15]. Furthermore, the choice of support material is 
very important for any process that involves supported catalysts. A 
carrier is usually a high-surface area inorganic material containing a 
complex pore structure through which catalytic materials are deposited. 
Earlier it was thought that support only provides a surface to disperse 
the catalytic substance to maximize the catalytic surface area. However, 
it is now quite clear that support can and often does play a critical role in 
maintaining the activity, selectivity, and durability of the finished 
catalyst. By far the most common carriers used in heterogeneous cata­
lysts are non-reducible inorganic oxides with a high surface area such as 
Al2O3, SiO2, TiO2, Al2O3-SiO2, ZrO2 etc. Al2O3 and SiO2owing to their 
excellent thermal and hydrothermal stability, low cost, and availability 
in desired purity with a well-controlled range of acidity are frequently 
employed as catalyst support. To facilitate easy access of bulk molecules 
to the active sites located within the pores, the support must exhibit a 
high surface area and large pore size. By changing the Al2O3/SiO2 ratios, 
Al2O3-SiO2 supports can be designed to meet the above objectives. 
Despite the advancements in the hydroprocessing of UCO, there is a 
gap in understanding the activity and selectivity of sulfided catalysts 
towards the production of jet-range (C9-C15) hydrocarbons through 
complex hydroconversion process chemistry. The previous studies have 
explored the catalytic hydroprocessing of UCO, revealing insights into 
conversion rates and product yields [16–24]. However, efficient cata­
lysts with extended lifetime and stability, especially for single-step HRJ 
production from UCO, remain underexplored. In addressing these gaps, 
the present study focuses on the hydroconversion of UCO over 
Ni-Mo/Al2O3-SiO2 catalysts in a fixed-bed reactor. Phosphorus is used as 
the second promoter in UCO hydroconversion catalyst. Phosphorus, a 
non-metallic element often overlooked in catalyst studies compared to 
metallic additives, plays a significant role in the NiMo/Al2O3-SiO2 
catalyst system. Research has shown that phosphorus can stabilize the 
structure of Al2O3 and prevent the phase transition of γ-Al2O3 to α-Al2O3 
at high temperatures [25]. When incorporated, phosphorus decorates 
the hydroxyl groups on the alumina surface, introducing P-OH groups 
[26] that alter the distribution of Lewis acid and Brønsted acid sites on 
the Al2O3 surface. This modification can have a profound impact on the 
catalytic properties and performance of the NiMo/Al2O3-SiO2 catalyst, 
influencing its activity and selectivity in the hydrodeoxygenation pro­
cess for converting feedstocks like used cooking oil into sustainable 
aviation fuel range hydrocarbons. The presence of phosphorus leads to 
an increase in the catalytic activity and reduction of deactivation [27]. 
The primary objective of the present study is to investigate the catalyst’s 
suitability in producing jet-range hydrocarbon molecules viz. paraffins, 
cycloparaffins, low aromatics with high yield, aligning with jet fuel 
specifications. This study not only contributes valuable information for 
optimizing hydrodeoxygenation catalyst formulation and hydro­
conversion process conditions but also introduces suitable hydro­
processing routes for producing Jet A-1 range aviation fuel from UCO 
ensuring longer lifetime and stability of catalyst.

2.2. Catalysts Preparation for Hydrodeoxygenation of used cooking oil 
The preparation of the catalyst for hydrodeoxygenation involves a 
two-stage process. The first stage focuses on synthesizing a porous 
support material. This support is composed of alumina (Al2O3) and silica 
(SiO2) with tunable Al/Si ratios. The sol-gel method is employed for this 
purpose, involving three distinct steps. and the second stage involves 
depositing nickel (Ni) and molybdenum (Mo) onto the prepared support 
to get the final catalyst. 
In the first stage, Al2O3-SiO2 support synthesis process involved three 
steps: (1) the synthesis of mixed sol; (2) the synthesis of wet gel and the 
process of aging treatment; (3) Supercritical ethanol drying. In step 1, 
alumina sol and silica sol were prepared separately by stirring the 
mixture of AlCl3⋅6 H2O/EtOH/ H2O = with the mole ratio of 1/12/35 
and TEOS/H2O/EtOH/HCl = with the mole ratio of 1/4/6/7.5×10−3 
respectively. Then both the sols were mixed in molar ratios of Al/Si = 1/ 
0, 2/1, 3/1 at 30◦C for 60 min to complete the hydrolysis. In step 2, 1,4- 
dioxane was added to the mixed sol to promote condensation. This was 
done by quickly stirring the mixture of sols at 30◦C for 15 min and sealed 
at room temperature to form the wet gel. In the process of aging treat­
ment, the wet gel was soaked in anhydrous ethanol at 50◦C to strengthen 
the network and swap out the redundant water and organic matter. 
In step 3, the wet gel was placed in an autoclave which was sealed 
and filled with anhydrous ethanol. To ensure safety requirements, the 
autoclave must be recharged with pure nitrogen gas to exhaust the ox­
ygen. The absolute pressure was controlled in the range of 0.1–9.1 MPa 
with the temperature increased to 270 ◦C at a rate of 2 ◦C/min. After the 
heat-preservation stage, the absolute pressure was slowly reduced to 
0.1 MPa by the slow release of the ethanol. Finally, the samples were 
obtained at room temperature. The serial numbers of samples, 90Al- 
10Si, and 70Al-30Si, corresponded to different mole ratios of Al/Si. 
Al2O3–SiO2 aerogel, samples were calcined at 550 ◦C respectively for 5 h 
with a rate of 3 ◦C/min. 
In the second stage for the preparation of the hydrodeoxygenation 
catalyst, the support γ-alumina (Trilobe extrudates) was first dried at 
150◦C for 2 hours and calcined at 550◦C for 5 hours to remove moisture. 
The pore volume was measured using the water-wetting technique. The 
Trilobe extrudates were then impregnated with a 0.5 wt% phosphorus 
solution (85 % H3PO4) using the incipient wetness method. This solution 
was sprayed onto the extrudates and air-dried for 12 hours at room 
temperature, followed by drying at 120◦C for 12 hours in an oven, and 
then calcined at 550◦C for 5 hours to produce a phosphorus- 
impregnated trilobe catalyst. Subsequently, 10 wt% molybdenum was

2.3. Preparation of hydroisomerization catalysts 
The preparation of hydroisomerization catalysts involves two main 
stages, the synthesis of ZSM-22 zeolite framework and the subsequent 
incorporation of platinum on template-free ZSM-22 zeolite. 
ZSM-22 samples (bulk molar SiAl = 45) were synthesized using 1,6- 
diaminohexane, NH2(CH2)6NH2 (Alfa Acer, 99 %), as a structure 
directing agent using the method reported elsewhere [28]. As per the 
method, the crystallization of ZSM-22 was performed using gel molar 
composition of 27NH2(CH2)6NH2/12 K2O/Al2O3/60SiO2/3670 H2O by 
employing potassium hydroxide, KOH (Merck, India); aluminum sulfate, 
Al2(SO4)3⋅18 H2O (Merck, India); and colloidal silica solution (LUDOX 
AS-40, Sigma-Aldrich) as precursors. Hydrothermal crystallization of 
the prepared gel was performed at 160◦C under stirred conditions for 
18 hours. The crystallized samples were then filtered, washed multiple 
times with deionized water, dried at 110◦C for 24 hours, and finally 
calcined at 550◦C for 12 hours in the presence of air to obtain the 
template-free ZSM-22 zeolite. The template-free zeolite was first 
ion-exchanged to its ammonium form using a 1 M ammonium nitrate 
solution under reflux conditions for 24 hours. The H-form of the zeolite 
was then obtained by calcining the NH4
+-zeolite at 600◦C for 6 hours 
under an airflow. The zeolite samples were subsequently loaded with 
0.5 wt% platinum using tetramine platinum (II) nitrate through an 
ion-exchange route, maintaining the solution pH around 10. The 
Pt-loaded zeolite sample (65 wt%) was then mixed with 35 wt% 
pseudo-boehmite and extruded into cylindrical extrudates of 1.6 mm 
diameter and approximately 3 mm length. These extrudates were 
calcined at 400◦C under a continuous flow of pure oxygen for 5 hours. 
The resulting Pt/H-ZSM-22 hydroisomerization catalyst had a bulk 
molar Si/Al ratio of 45. 
2.4. Catalyst characterization techniques 
The supports and catalysts were characterized by different tech­
niques. The nitrogen (N2) adsorption-desorption profiles of catalysts 
were measured using a Micromeritics ASAP-2020 plus surface area and 
porosity analyzer to derive the Brunauer–Emmett–Teller (BET) surface 
area (SBET), while the total pore volume (Vp) and average pore diam­
eter were evaluated using the Barrett–Joyner–Halenda method. The X- 
ray diffraction (XRD) patterns were recorded on a Panalytical AERIS X- 
ray diffractometer with Cu Kα emission (λ = 1.5406 Å) operating at 
40 kV and 40 mA. The framework acidity of all the samples was esti­
mated using ammonia temperature-programmed desorption (TPD) 
technique (Micromeritics Autochem Chemisorption analyzer). Typi­
cally, the samples were activated under helium flow at 300 ◦C for 1 h, 
followed by adsorption of ammonia at 100 ◦C using a gas mixture of 6 % 
ammonia in helium at a constant flow rate of 25 mL/min. A higher 
adsorption temperature was selected to minimize the extent of physical 
adsorption. Furthermore, to ensure the removal of any physically 
adsorbed ammonia, the sample was purged at 150 ◦C for 2 h under a 
flow rate of 50 mL/min of high-purity helium (99.99 %). TPD of 
ammonia was performed by heating the sample in the temperature 
range 150–900 ◦C, at a rate of 10 ◦C/min and under a helium flow rate of 
25 mL/min. The amount of ammonia desorbed from the sample was 
determined using a thermal conductivity detector (TCD) and was used to 
quantify acidity in terms of micromoles of ammonia desorbed per gram 
of zeolite sample. 
2.5. Pretreatment of the used cooking oil (UCOs) 
Used cooking oil (UCO) underwent a multi-step purification process 
to remove impurities and Free Fatty Acids (FFAs). The initial step 
involved filtering the untreated Used Cooking Oil (UCO) to eliminate 
waste residue, followed by centrifugation to separate the oil layer using 
a washing technique (hot water extraction method). Subsequently, the 
oil was aerated to induce bubbling in a separation funnel, facilitating the 
separation of FFAs from the oil layer. The resulting water/oil mixture 
underwent phase separation, yielding three distinct phases (water, FFAs, 
and oils). 
Finally, the purified oil layer, devoid of FFAs, was isolated and 
subjected to evaporation to obtain the pretreated UCO, referred to 
simply as UCO for the subsequent hydrodeoxygenation reactions. This 
meticulous pretreatment process ensures the removal of impurities and 
prepares the UCO for efficient utilization in further chemical 
transformations.
