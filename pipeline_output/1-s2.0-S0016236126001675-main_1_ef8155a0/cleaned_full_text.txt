A B S T R A C T
Faced with the urgent need for deep decarbonization in the aviation sector, Sustainable Aviation Fuel (SAF) 
derived from waste cooking oil (WCO) has emerged as a highly promising pathway. This review systematically 
examines the two most prominent technological routes for converting WCO into SAF: the commercially estab­
lished Hydroprocessing of Esters and Fatty Acids (HEFA) process and the emerging Catalytic Hydrothermolysis 
Jet (CHJ) process. Central to both pathways is the role of catalysts, which govern reaction efficiency, product 
distribution, and overall process economics. This paper provides a comprehensive analysis of recent advances in 
catalyst design, with a particular focus on evaluating the impact of key process parameters (temperature, 
pressure, hydrogen-to-oil ratio, and liquid hourly space velocity) on reaction pathways, product selectivity, and 
catalyst stability. Despite persistent challenges in catalyst durability, feedstock impurities, and process integra­
tion, WCO-based SAF demonstrates significant lifecycle carbon reduction potential, abundant feedstock avail­
ability, and avoidance of food fuel competition. With ongoing technological improvements and strong policy 
support, WCO-to-SAF conversion holds substantial promise for advancing the green transformation of the 
aviation industry.
1. Introduction
1.1. Background
Given the excessive greenhouse gas emissions and the aviation 
industry's heavy reliance on traditional petroleum jet fuel, sustainable 
development has become a global concern. Decarbonization and emis­
sion reduction stand out as major challenges for this sector. Global ini­
tiatives are underway to realize carbon neutrality across all industries by 
2050. Although aviation accounted for only 2–3% of global carbon 
emissions in 2019, its CO2 emissions are projected to rise with growing 
aviation demand[1]. Furthermore, aviation activities generate non-CO2 
emissions, such as climate-damaging ozone and contrails[2]. Alternative 
aviation fuels, particularly Sustainable Aviation Fuel (SAF), which can 
significantly reduce both greenhouse gas (GHG) and non-CO2 emissions, 
are being intensively developed to replace conventional aviation fuels 
and are a key pathway to achieving carbon neutrality goals[3].
The increase in human consumption of plant and animal oils has led 
to the rise in waste cooking oil (WCO) production. Rather than being 
disposed of, WCO can be technologically converted into a sustainable 
feedstock for biofuel production, thereby maximizing the value of bio­
wastes through energy recovery while addressing disposal issues[4,5]. 
China ranks among the world's most abundant sources of WCO, with an 
estimated annual production of approximately 10 million tons[6]. 
However, due to an underdeveloped recycling system, the standardized 
recovery rate remains only about 50%. This waste cooking oil primarily 
originates from the catering industry and household discharges. Its main 
components are triglycerides (TG) and fatty acids (FA)[7], resulting in 
elevated acid value and oxygen content. Table 1 provides a comparative 
analysis of the key characteristics of waste cooking oil and fresh cooking 
oil. During the frying process, WCO becomes contaminated with various 
derivatives, including free fatty acids (FFA), volatile compounds (e.g.,

Policy support is a key driver for SAF adoption. Under the ReFuelEU 
Aviation initiative, starting January 1, 2025, all aviation fuel supplied at 
EU airports must contain a minimum of 2% SAF, rising to 6% by 2030 
and 70% by 2050[12]. Similarly, the UK House of Commons has 
approved the Renewable Transport Fuel Obligation (Sustainable Avia­
tion Fuel) Order 2024, which is expected to provide the UK aviation 
industry with access to approximately 1.2 million tons of SAF annually. 
In China, policy frameworks like the New Mechanism for Green and 
Low-Carbon Energy Transition explicitly support the R&D and industrial 
application of SAF technology, providing institutional safeguards for the 
aviation sector's low-carbon transition. According to the International 
Air Transport Association (IATA), over 45 airlines worldwide have now 
used Bio-jet fuel. Driven by these synergistic policy and market forces, a 
global SAF supply chain is rapidly emerging.
This paper reviews the research progress and industrial development 
of SAF derived from WCO, focusing on mainstream preparation tech­
nologies and key catalytic reactions, as well as the design, performance 
optimization, and industrial application of core catalysts. By systemat­
ically synthesizing relevant literature, policies, and industrial practice 
data, this work aims to clarify the technical bottlenecks and challenges 
in current WCO-to-SAF conversion pathway and to outline critical di­
rections for future technological innovation and industrial upgrading.

2. Principles and processes of Bio-jet from waste cooking oil
2.1. Overview of waste cooking oil
2.1.1. Physical and chemical properties of waste cooking oil
Waste cooking oil is a complex mixture composed of fats and oils 
whose physicochemical properties differ significantly from those of 
refined vegetable oils, primarily due to high acid value, high viscosity, 
and a complex composition of impurities. WCO exhibits elevated acid 
values due to the hydrolytic production of free fatty acids (FFA), which 
degrade flow properties, cause equipment corrosion, and accelerate 
oxidative side reactions[13]. Moreover, waste cooking oil contains high 
oxygen content due to the glycerol skeleton and acyl part of the mole­
cule[14]. This high oxygen content in the feedstock for aviation fuels 
results in poor compatibility with fossil fuels and increased viscosity 
(limiting blending potential and worsening cold flow properties), as well 
as high reactivity and poor stability. These factors contribute to poly­
merization, leading to the formation of gums and sludge, as well as 
reduced thermal and storage stability[15,16]. Despite these challenges, 
WCO has a high calorific value, and its low sulfur content and renewable 
nature make it a significant potential feedstock for producing SAF. 
However, it requires a series of processing steps to achieve the standard 
performance of conventional aviation fuel[17,18].

2.1.2. Waste cooking oil used as Sustainable aviation fuel raw material
The feedstock system of Bio-jet fuel is diversified and can usually be 
divided into three main generations[19]. The first generation of feed­
stocks are mainly food crops (e.g. corn, sugar cane); the second gener­
ation of feedstocks covers non-food energy crops (e.g. jatropha, castor) 
and waste biomass (e.g. waste cooking oil, agricultural and forestry 
residues); and the third generation of feedstocks focuses on algae re­
sources[19].
Waste cooking oil represents a leading candidate among second- 
generation feedstocks, demonstrating considerable potential for sus­
tainable energy production. Its current large-scale applications have 
been primarily in biodiesel production. However, in the field of SAF, 
WCO is becoming one of the core feedstocks for the hydrogenation 
(HEFA) route, due to its well-established supply chain and significant 
carbon reduction potential[21]. Hydrotreating technologies are being 
revolutionized globally, with the development of new reaction systems 
such as the "one-pot method", which aims to reduce energy consumption 
by about 15–20% compared to the traditional two-stage hydrotreating 
process (hydrodeoxygenation-hydroisomerization/cracking) by inte­
grating multiple reaction steps[22]. Meanwhile, key qualities such as 
yield and cold flow performance of Bio-jet fuel are also being improved 
through high-throughput screening and rational design optimization of 
catalysts (e.g., development of non-precious-metal catalysts, modulation 
of carrier acidity, etc.)[21]. From a full life cycle perspective, waste 
cooking oil shows multi-dimensional advantages in Sustainable Aviation 
Fuel production:
(1) Resource recycling and sustainability: WCO is generated from 
kitchen waste, which has a huge annual potential, and the standardized 
recycling rate of used cooking oil in China is even less than 10% [23]. 
Converting it into aviation fuel through resource utilization can effec­
tively solve the "waste oil disposal dilemma" and food safety problems, 
and avoid competing with food crops for arable land resources, which is 
a perfect fit for the strategic requirements of the circular economy and 
low-carbon transformation[20].
(2) Economic viability: Raw material costs are key to the economics 
of Bio-jet fuel. WCO's raw material costs can be significantly lower than 
conventional vegetable oils such as palm oil and soybean oil, and 
because it is derived from waste, it requires no additional land cultiva­
tion or agricultural inputs, resulting in a significant life-cycle cost 
advantage[24].
(3) In terms of environmental benefits, WCO-derived aviation fuel 
can reduce CO2 by over 80% throughout its entire lifecycle compared to 
coal-to-jet fuel (CJF) [25–27]. In addition, WCO fuels have ultra-low 
sulfur characteristics (less than 1 ppm sulfur), much lower than con­
ventional aviation kerosene (about 500–1000 ppm)[28]. During com­
bustion, WCO-based fuels have a higher hydrogen to carbon ratio (H/C) 
and the concentration of aromatic hydrocarbon compounds is substan­
tially reduced through hydrotreating processes, which effectively elim­
inate oxygenated functional groups and saturate unsaturated carbon 
bonds, resulting in a final hydrocarbon product with lower particulate 
matter (PM) emissions and a significant improvement in air quality in 
the airport area[21].
2.2. Preparation process for conversion of waste cooking oil to Bio-jet fuel
According to the latest ASTM D7566 standard (as of early 2026), the 
internationally recognized production pathways for Sustainable Avia­
tion Fuel are currently categorized into eight major types, including 
Hydroprocessed Esters and Fatty Acids (HEFA), Fischer-Tropsch (FT), 
Alcohol-to-Jet (AtJ), Synthesized Iso-Paraffins (SIP), etc [26,29]. These 
pathways each possess distinct characteristics in terms of feedstock 
adaptability, technological maturity, carbon emission intensity, and 
economic viability[30]. However, for liquid biomass feedstocks like 
waste cooking oil, which exhibit high oxygen content, high acid value, 
and complex composition, the HEFA and CHJ pathways demonstrate 
unique advantages and industrial scalability[31]. Among these, HEFA is 
currently the only SAF production technology using oils and fats as 
feedstock that has achieved large-scale industrial application. Mean­
while, CHJ, as an emerging technology, is undergoing extensive exper­
imental research due to its low hydrogen consumption and high-quality 
Bio-jet fuel characteristics. Although both HEFA and CHJ convert fatty 
acids and fatty acid esters from feedstocks into jet fuel, their conversion 
pathways differ: The HEFA process converts esters and/or fatty acids 
into hydrocarbons through dehydrogenation, hydrocracking, and 
hydroisomerization[16,32]. The CHJ process relies on hydrothermal 
liquefaction reactors and hydrogenation reactions to produce kerosene 
[32,33]. The HEFA pathway is recognized as the most technologically 
mature and commercially established approach sanctioned by the

2.2.1. Hydroprocessing of esters and fatty acids (HEFA)
The typical process flow of the HEFA process is shown in Fig. 3. 
Firstly, raw materials such as waste cooking oil are pretreated by 
physical filtration, dehydration and deacidification to remove impurities 
and free fatty acids. Subsequently, the pre-treated oils and fats are 
subjected to catalytic hydrogenation over catalysts (e.g., H-ZSM-5)[34] 
at high temperatures (250–400◦C) and high pressures (3–10 MPa). This 
process primarily converts saturated fatty acid triglycerides into C15-C18 
straight-chain alkanes (i.e., hydrogenated vegetable oil) via the hydro­
deoxygenation (HDO) pathway, simultaneously producing water and 
propane byproducts. To meet aviation fuel performance requirements, 
the resulting straight-chain alkanes require further hydroisomerization 
and selective cracking[35]. The final product is a bio-aviation fuel (C8- 
C16) compliant with ASTM D7566 standards[36], where the precise 
carbon number distribution can be achieved by modulating the catalyst 
acidity, metal active sites, and process parameters (e.g., temperature 
and pressure).
In terms of mechanism, the process is essentially a coupling of the 
targeted removal of oxygen atoms with carbon skeleton remodeling and 
is roughly divided into two steps. The first step is the conversion of 
double bonds in fatty acid chains to saturated fatty acids by catalytic 
hydrogenation with the addition of molecular hydrogen (Fig. 4a)[16], 
followed by the hydrodeoxygenation, decarboxylation, and decarbon­
ylation of triacylglycerols. Hydrogenation of triacylglycerols is usually 
realized by β-elimination (Fig. 4b), and in addition to this it also may be 
realized by two different reaction pathways (γ-hydrogen migration, 
β-elimination and direct deoxygenation) (Fig. 4b)[37]. Within the 
decarboxylation pathway, the carboxylic acid moiety is cleaved from the 
triglyceride chain by direct scission of the C–C bond under H2 atmo­
sphere, and feedstock deoxygenation is achieved by the elimination of 
O2 and the release of the byproduct, CO2 (Fig. 4c). Analogously, in the 
decarbonylation route, the carboxyl moiety is cleaved from the triglyc­
eride chain through direct C–C bond scission under H2 atmosphere, 
leading to feedstock deoxygenation via O2 elimination and the release of 
CO and H2O as characteristic by-products (Fig. 4c)[16].
The second step involves hydroisomerization and hydrocracking 
reactions, where the feedstock from the first step is converted into 
straight-chain alkanes with carbon chain lengths exceeding C16. N-al­
kanes usually have the disadvantages of low octane number (poor gas­
oline detonation resistance) and high freezing point (poor low- 
temperature fluidity in diesel and lubricating oils), and in order to 
improve the quality of the fuels, it is necessary to subject the generated 
hydrocarbons to hydrocracking and hydroisomerization treatments 
[38,39]. The hydrocracking and hydroisomerization reactions are 
typically carried out over a bifunctional catalyst and Fig. 5 shows the 
reaction mechanism of hydroisomerisation and hydrocracking of n-al­
kanes. The n-alkane molecules adsorb onto the metal sites of the catalyst 
to undergo dehydrogenation, losing two hydrogen atoms (H2) to form 
the corresponding n-alkenes (A).
The generated alkene molecule diffuses away from the metal site and 
adsorbs onto a neighboring acidic site. A proton (H+) adds to the alkene 
double bond, forming a high-energy n-alkyl carbonium ion (B). Side 
reactions also occur during this step: n-alkanes directly accept protons at 
acidic sites to form n-alkyl carbonylate ion (C); subsequent dehydroge­
nation yields n-alkyl carbonium ion (D). At this point, the reaction enters 
the core transformation pathway. The straight-chain n-alkyl carbenium 
ion undergoes a skeletal rearrangement via hydrogen migration or 
methyl migration, yielding a branched alkyl carbenium ion (mono­
branched, dibranched, tribranched) (F). When the branch position of 
alkyl carbenium ion (especially a multi-branched one) satisfies the 
conditions, C–C bond cleavage (β-cleavage) occurs, yielding x-alkenes 
and (n-x)-branched alkyl carbonium ion (G). The x-alkenes formed by 
cracking undergo hydrogenation at the metal site to produce a short- 
chain alkane (meeting aviation fuel carbon number requirements) (E). 
The (n-x)-branched alkyl carbonium ion undergoes "deprotonation" to 
form a branched-chain alkene, which is subsequently hydrogenated at 
the metal site to yield a branched-chain alkane (H)[38,40].
At present, although the technology of preparing Bio-jet fuel from 
bio-oils and fats through the traditional two-step process (hydro­
deoxygenation to generate straight-chain alkanes, followed by isomer­
ization/cracking) has been industrialized globally, its inherent defects, 
such as a long process flow, high reactor investment cost, and high 
consumption of hydrogen, have significantly raised the production cost. 
For this reason, the one-pot method, with its potential for simplified 
processes and energy savings, has become the focus of research. The core 
of this technology lies in the use of a multifunctional catalyst that cou­
ples multiple reaction steps, such as hydrodeoxygenation, hydro­
isomerization and selective cracking, in a single reactor. As shown in 
Fig. 6, the refined animal and vegetable fats and oils undergo hydrogen 
saturation, hydrodeoxygenation, alkane isomerization, and selective 
cracking under the action of bifunctional catalysts, and the standardized 
Bio-jet fuel are obtained directly through fractional distillation. The 
success of this process is critically dependent on the design of a high- 
performance bifunctional catalyst that enables simultaneous hydro­
deoxygenation, cracking and isomerization activities, as well as good 
stability and anti-coking properties. This process is mainly in the 
research and development stage due to the challenging catalyst design, 
susceptibility to inactivation and the complexity and difficulty of the 
reaction network.

macromolecular polymeric organics in biomass are hydrolyzed, depo­

solvent, methanol/ethanol could also be used as a solvent for the con­

is now widely used for bio-oil conversion[44,45].
The CHJ process primarily consists of four steps, as shown in Fig. 7. 
First, the raw materials undergo pretreatment. The pretreated oil enters 
the hydrothermal liquefaction reactor, where components such as tri­
glycerides in waste cooking oil undergo hydrolysis, deoxygenation, 
decarboxylation, and polymerization reactions under the action of su­
percritical water and catalysts. These reactions convert the oil into 
lower-molecular-weight biocrude[41]. The biocrude is then pumped 
into a hydrogenation reactor where, under the action of a catalyst, it 
undergoes hydrogenation deoxygenation (HDO) upgrading treatment at 
high temperature, high pressure, and in a hydrogen atmosphere. Finally, 
through atmospheric or vacuum distillation, products such as naphtha, 
jet fuel fractions, and diesel are separated based on boiling point dif­
ferences[19,46,47].
As an emerging technology, CHJ currently operates at the pilot and 
demonstration stages in terms of technical maturity, with few large-scale 
industrial applications. Its core advantage lies in significantly lower 
hydrogen consumption compared to HEFA. Unlike the HEFA process, 
CHJ achieves efficient deoxygenation through radical reaction mecha­
nisms in a hydrothermal environment, resulting in higher aromatic 
content in the product and thereby enhancing fuel thermal stability. 
However, catalyst longevity faces severe challenges: the hydrothermal 
reaction environment (high-temperature, high-pressure water) demands 
exceptional hydrothermal stability from catalysts, with leaching of 
metal active components and collapse of the support structure being the 
primary causes of deactivation[48,49]. Table 2 shows the comparison of 
the analyses of HEFA and CHJ.

The essence of isomerization-cracking catalysts lies in their dual- 
functionality, with performance highly dependent on the balance be­
tween metallic and acidic functions. This process selectively converts 
long-chain n-alkanes into high-value branched isomers and small- 
molecule hydrocarbons. The metal active sites and acidic sites within 
the isomerization-cracking catalyst exhibit synergistic effects. These 
metal sites typically employ precious metals such as platinum or palla­
dium, while the acidic sites utilize supports like zeolite molecular sieves, 
modified alumina, and alumina[21,31]. The following is an analysis and 
comparison of different types of catalysts (Table 3).

active metals to bimetallic systems. Their high performance in bio-oil 
HDO reactions stems from the synergistic regulation of dual-functional

metal-support interactions. This multiscale regulatory mechanism 
directly determines the catalyst's intrinsic HDO activity, selectivity for 
target hydrocarbon products, and long-term operational stability[52].

effects. Its core advantage lies in achieving precise functional division

oxygen affinity, selectively adsorb and polarize C-O bonds in fatty acid 
molecules, laying the groundwork for deoxygenation reactions. Mean­

Assessing the relative advantages and industrial potential of different 
catalyst systems requires in-depth investigation into their structur­

Yan et al. prepared Ni-La/SiO2 catalysts via the coprecipitation method. 
Within the system, La3+ induces strong metal-support interactions, 
acting as a "structural aid" to stabilize highly dispersed Ni nanoparticles 
and effectively suppress their sintering during high-temperature re­
actions. This enhanced physical stability directly correlates with the 
catalyst's sustained deoxygenation activity, enabling the efficient and 
highly selective conversion of fatty acid methyl esters into long-chain 
alkanes[55]. Similarly, the NiMo/SiO2-TiO2 catalyst developed by Lin 
Fu et al. demonstrates dual synergistic effects between the support and 
metal: the mesoporous SiO2-TiO2 composite support not only provides 
tunable acidic sites, but its TiO2 component further promotes the 
dispersion and stability of metal nanoparticles through strong metal- 
support interactions (SMSI). Simultaneously, the introduction of Mo 
species optimizes the active sites' adsorption and polarization capabil­
ities for C=O/C-O bonds in oxygen-containing intermediates. Syner­

reaction pathway toward hydrogenation-deoxygenation, yielding high-

excellent resistance to metal poisoning and chlorine attack, resulting in a 
robust anti-poisoning catalyst system. In the same way, the ZrO2/TiO2 
composite oxide carrier demonstrated outstanding sulfur tolerance and 
regeneration performance during the catalytic process[57]. In the 
precious-metal/non-precious-metal system, the Pt-Ni/CWMM-CO cata­
lyst synthesized by Fenti Visiamah et al. achieved a 33.1% aviation 
kerosene hydrocarbon yield (with 99.99% selectivity for C8–C16 hy­

indicates that bimetallic catalysts not only enhance hydrogenation ac­
tivity but also generate additional acidic sites at the metal-support 
interface, thereby simultaneously increasing both the rate of aromatic 
hydrogenation and the rate of oxygen removal[59].
Shinyoung Oh et al. prepared three nickel-based bimetallic catalysts 
on hexagonally structured mesoporous SBA-15 carriers (NiCu/ SBA-15, 
NiMn/SBA-15, and NiZn/SBA-15) and comparatively analyzed the ef­
fects of single and bimetallic on bio-oil hydrodeoxygenation activity. 
Compared to the single-metal Ni/SBA-15 catalyst, the bimetallic design 
significantly alters the properties of Ni active sites through electronic 
effects, generally reducing light oil yield while substantially increasing 
the proportion of gas products[60,61].
In addition to adjusting the intrinsic composition of bimetallic sys­
tems, surface functionalization of the support is another effective 
strategy for regulating the microenvironment of active sites. For 
example, the research by Noor Azira Abdul Razak et al. provides a 
typical case: they functionalized SBA-15 mesoporous molecular sieves 
with sulfhydryl (−SH) groups and then loaded them with a Ni-Co 
bimetallic compound. The core of this design lies in the −SH groups 
acting as strong anchoring sites, effectively enhancing the dispersion of 
the metal precursor and suppressing its sintering during reduction and 
reaction processes. Characterization confirmed that the obtained Ni-Co/ 
SBA-15-SH catalyst exhibits higher metal dispersion and tunable acidity 
while maintaining its hexagonal mesoporous structure. Consequently, 
this catalyst demonstrated significantly optimized performance in the 
hydrogenation deoxygenation of WCO, achieving not only an overall 
hydrocarbon yield of 80% but, more critically, a selectivity of up to 92% 
for the target jet fuel fraction (C8-C16)[62]. Complementary to this, 
Darfizzi Deraw et al prepared novel bimetallic NiCo/SBA-15-NH2 cata­
lysts by loading nickel and cobalt onto amino-functionalized SBA-15 
mesoporous carriers by the wet impregnation method, and the charac­
terization showed that the catalysts maintained a hexagonal mesoporous 
structure (specific surface area 393.93 m2/g), the metal particles were 
uniformly dispersed and formed weak/strong acid synergistic sites. The 
introduction of –NH2 to enhance the Lewis acid sites promoted decar­
boxylation and C–C bond cleavage, and 83% C8-C20 hydrocarbon yield 
and 88% jet fuel selectivity were achieved under optimized conditions 
(330◦C, 5% catalyst loading, 300 min), with the main products being 
C15-C17 alkanes/olefins. The above two Ni-Co bimetallic systems (Ni- 
Co/SBA-15-SH) synergistically enhance the deoxygenation efficiency 
through electronic and geometrical effects, and the sulfur-modified 
(−SH) SBA-15 carrier enhances the metal dispersion and inhibits the 
carbon accumulation. The SBA-15 carrier modified by an amino group 
(NH2) enhances the dispersion of Ni-Co alloy (particle size of 5 nm) and 
inhibits excessive cracking[63].
Bimetallic catalysts, through multi-metal synergistic effects and 
support engineering, enhance hydrogenation deoxygenation activity 
and selectivity while also facing inherent trade-offs such as product 
distribution regulation (e.g., excessive cracking)[60]. Its industrial po­
tential depends not only on intrinsic activity but also on resistance to 
poisoning, long-term stability, and the economic balance of non- 
precious metal substitutions[56]. Therefore, rational catalyst design 
requires synergistic optimization of the active site microenvironment 
and cost-effectiveness to drive practical applications.
3.1.2. Bifunctional catalysts
Beyond bimetallic catalysts, the precise "spatial configuration 
design" of metal sites and acidic sites represents another cutting-edge 
strategy for achieving highly efficient and selective synergistic catal­
ysis. For example, Tinghu Wang et al prepared Ni/Beta bifunctional 
catalysts for the preparation of bio-aviation coal from the hydro­
deoxygenation of methyl lauric acid to achieve the highest conversion 
(81%) and C11-C12 alkane selectivity (46.5%)[64]. Xincheng Cao et al 
reported a stable and efficient HDO catalyst (Pd@Alx-mSiO2) with a 
uniformly dispersed palladium (Pd) core inside and a mesoporous silica 
shell doped with aluminum (Al) atoms on the outside. Al doping was 
introduced into the Lewis and Brønsted acid sites, which synergistically 
acted with Pd, and resulted in a methyl palmitate conversion of up to 
98% and the alkane selectivity was > 99%[65]. Moreover, mesoporous 
silica in catalysts combines the advantages of SiO2—resistance to sulfur, 
chlorine, and metal poisoning—with its own inherent strengths, making 
it one of the carriers with the strongest overall resistance to impurities 
currently available. Shengzhe Ding et al reported a Pd-doped multistage- 
pore ZSM-5 catalyst with Pd nanoparticles selectively loaded in meso­
pores and acid sites located in micropores, which were spatially isolated 
to avoid side reactions. In lauric acid hydrodeoxygenation, the dodecane 
generation efficiency was 8.5 times higher than that of conventional 
catalysts[34]. The fundamental purpose of these catalysts is to maximize 
the efficiency of the main reaction by precisely controlling the molecular 
transport pathways and reaction sequences of the reactants. This marks 
a shift in catalyst design from traditional "material screening" to a new 
phase of "system construction", providing a core approach for devel­
oping next-generation customized catalysts for high-performance bio­
refineries in the future.
Bifunctional catalysts integrate metallic sites (responsible for hy­
drogenation/dehydrogenation) with acidic sites (responsible for isom­
erization, cracking, and dehydration), enabling them to catalyze 
multiple sequential reactions in series. This dual functionality is pivotal 
for achieving a one-step hydrogenation-deoxygenation-isomerization 
process for bio-oils. The core of their design lies in the precise matching 
and synergistic interaction between metallic and acidic functionalities. 
For example, the classic Pt/SAPO-11[66] and Ni/ZSM-5[67] catalysts 
utilize precious metals or non-precious metals to provide hydrogenation 
activity, respectively, and rely on the shape-selective acidic channels of 
molecular sieves to direct isomerization reactions[67]. Such catalysts 
combine hydrogenation activity with isomerization capability, enabling

3.1.3. Molecular sieve catalysts
The realization of high-performance catalysts relies not only on the 
optimization of active metal sites (such as bimetallic synergism) but also 
on the precise design of synergistic "acid-functional" carriers. In recent 
years, molecular sieves have received wide attention and applications as 
inorganic crystalline materials (e.g., zeolite molecular sieves, aluminum 
phosphate molecular sieves, etc.) or organic framework materials (e.g., 
MOFs, COFs) with regular and uniform pore structure (usually micro­
porous, < 2 nm), as well as a bright application prospect in the field of 
catalysis. Zeolite molecular sieves are crystals composed of silicates or 
aluminosilicates, whose oxide-based framework consists of tetrahedra 
sharing corners, where T denotes the tetrahedral atom, most commonly 
Si and Al. Different tetrahedral connectivity patterns result in distinct 
zeolite framework types[69]. This material exhibits unique shape 
selectivity, high thermal stability, and strong acidity. Its molecular 
structure promotes the diffusion of reactants and products, making it 
suitable for hydrocracking and hydroisomerization reactions[70].
To date, 235 distinct zeolite framework types have been identified in 
natural or synthetic zeolites. Brønsted sites and Lewis sites are the two 
most common active sites in zeolite-mediated material transformation 
reactions[40], e.g. The reaction mechanism for biomass conversion at 
Brønsted acid sites involves bridging hydroxyl groups (Si-OH-Al) within 
the zeolite framework providing protons (H+) to catalyze dehydration, 
condensation, and alkylation reactions. Electron-deficient metal centers 
within the framework (e.g., Sn4+ and Ti4+) catalyze redox reactions and 
molecular rearrangements by accepting electron pairs, representing 
biomass conversion reactions at Lewis acid sites.
Among numerous zeolite molecular sieves, H-ZSM-5 is a zeolite 
material featuring a three-dimensional decagonal ring cross-linked pore 
structure. Its interior contains two intersecting pore systems with a 
stable framework. Due to its strong acidity, direct application in the 
conversion of long-chain alkanes often leads to excessive cracking. 
Research efforts therefore employ various modification strategies to 
balance its dehydrogenation activity with selectivity toward target 
products. For example, Indriana Kartini et al. demonstrated that Mo- 
loaded H-ZSM-5 can be used for the dehydrogenation of palm kernel 
oil. Its acidic sites synergize with metal sites to yield highly selective 
C8–C16 hydrocarbon fractions[71]. Hartati et al. synthesized a Ca/ZSM- 
5 support using volcanic ash and marble waste, then loaded Ni to form a 
bifunctional catalyst. The introduced Ca2+ provided basic sites that 
effectively neutralized some strong acid centers, suppressing cracking 
side reactions. This significantly enhanced the selectivity of C15–C17 
long-chain hydrocarbons in the deoxygenation products of waste cook­
ing oil by 18.9% compared to unmodified ZSM-5, reaching 66.6%[72]. 
This directly demonstrates that altering the acidity of the carrier through 
chemical modification is an effective means of controlling product 
distribution.
SAPO-11 (AEL) is a prominent member of the aluminium silicate- 
phosphate molecular sieve family. With its unique one-dimensional 
decahydrocyclohexene ring structure and adjustable moderate acidity, 
it holds an almost irreplaceable position in the selective isomerization of 
long-chain n-alkanes, minimizing cracking side reactions to the greatest 
extent. SAPO catalysts for alkane hydroisomerization are usually sup­
plemented with Pd and Pt to obtain high activity and selectivity[73]. For 
example, the SAPO-11 molecular sieve prepared by Yang, Sanni et al. 
achieved a conversion rate of up to 81.8% for n-hexadecane and an 
isomerization selectivity of 86.5% under optimized conditions[66]. This 
study clearly demonstrates the high efficiency of the tandem catalytic 
cycle formed by Pt sites (dehydrogenation/hydrogenation) and SAPO- 
11 acid sites (isomerization).
In developing bifunctional catalysts for the hydrogenation- 
deoxygenation and isomerization of bio-oils, the rational selection and 
modification of acidic supports are crucial. The core design principle 
involves selecting a fundamental carrier based on the target reaction. 
Through methods such as metal loading, ion exchange, heteroatom 
doping, or structural engineering, the type, strength, and distribution of 
the carrier's acidity are precisely controlled to match the characteristics 
of the metal active sites. This enables the efficient and highly selective 
conversion of bio-oils into target fuel components. In industrial appli­
cations, molecular sieves exhibit excellent thermal stability. However, 
achieving precise control over their acid distribution and pore structure 
at scale is costly. It is also sensitive to impurities in raw materials (H2O, 
S), and its applicability depends on the balance between its resistance to 
poisoning and the economic viability of modification.
3.1.4. Other catalysts
Catalyst design is no longer confined to traditional molecular sieves; 
other materials with tunable acidity or ordered structures are also being 
extensively explored. The phosphate-modified nano-zirconia catalyst 
reported by Saviola et al. achieved a Bio-jet fuel selectivity of 33.92% in 
the hydrogenation conversion of waste palm oil by forming synergistic 
acidic sites comprising P–OH (Brønsted acid) and Zr4+ (Lewis acid)[74]. 
Metal-organic framework-derived materials (MOFs) or carbon-based 
composites derived from them as precursors demonstrate immense po­
tential in biomass conversion reactions such as furfural hydrogenation 
and triglyceride hydrogenation. Their ultra-high specific surface area 
and customizable pore/metal site architecture offer novel insights for 
designing novel bifunctional catalysts[75–77]. Carbon-based materials, 
with their exceptional chemical inertness, corrosion resistance, hydro­
phobicity, and highly tunable pore structure, effectively resist chemical 
erosion from sulfur and chlorine, mitigate the effects of alkali metals, 
and buffer carbon deposits. They have been experimentally proven to be 
a highly stable carrier choice for treating WCO[78].
3.2. Catalyst for catalytic Hydrothermolysis jet
The CHJ process comprises two distinct catalytic stages: initial Hy­
drothermal Liquefaction (HTL) and subsequent Hydrodeoxygenation 
(HDO) upgrading[87]. The catalyst requirements and challenges differ 
significantly between these stages and from those in the HEFA process, 
particularly concerning the harsh hydrothermal environment.
3.2.1. Catalysts for the hydrothermal liquefaction (HTL) stage
Under specific conditions, the HTL process can proceed without a 
catalyst. However, catalysts can significantly enhance reaction rates, 
increase biological yield, and reduce oxygen content under milder 
conditions[88]. The catalyst used in this stage must be capable of 
withstanding high-temperature, high-pressure water[89]. Alkali cata­
lysts (e.g., K2CO3, Na2CO3, KOH) are widely used. They promote the 
hydrolysis of triglycerides into fatty acids and glycerol, and facilitate

decarboxylation/dehydrogenation reactions, thereby increasing bio­
crude yield and reducing its viscosity and oxygen content[90]. Organic 
acids (e.g., formic, acetic acid) can act as in-situ hydrogen donors, aiding 
stabilization of reactive intermediates. Solid catalysts offer easier sepa­
ration and potential reuse. Common types include: Zeolites (e.g., H- 
ZSM-5), metal oxides (e.g., CaO, ZrO2). They catalyze cracking, dehy­
dration, and ketonization reactions. Additionally, Redox-active metal

removal of oxygen as CO or CO2. The choice of HTL catalyst depends on 
the feedstock and desired biocrude properties. A key trade-off is that 
while catalysts can improve biocrude quality, they may also suffer from 
deactivation due to coking or metal leaching in the aggressive hydro­
thermal medium.
3.2.2. Catalysts for the hydrodeoxygenation (HDO) upgrading stage
The biocrude from HTL requires further HDO to produce 
specification-compliant jet fuel. The catalyst employed here is concep­
tually similar to the HDO step in the HEFA process, requiring a combi­
nation of metal sites (for hydrogenation) and acid sites (for dehydration 
and cracking) [91]. However, the feedstock (biocrude) is more complex 
than pure triglycerides, containing a wider range of oxygenates, unsat­
urated compounds, and often higher nitrogen and sulfur content. The 
appropriate catalyst should be selected based on the feedstock charac­
teristics and process objectives.
For traditional HDO catalysts, Transition metal sulfide catalysts (e.g., 
NiMoS/γ-Al2O3, CoMoS/γ-Al2O3) are effective for the simultaneous 
removal of oxygen, sulfur, and nitrogen. Noble metals (Pt, Pd) on 
various supports are also active but more sensitive to impurities[92]. 
Research is exploring tailored catalysts for biocrude upgrading. For 
instance, iron-based catalysts (Fe2O3/γ-Al2O3) have shown promise due 
to their redox properties, promoting decarboxylation pathways[47]. 
Nickel supported on ordered mesoporous silica (e.g., Ni/SBA-15) has 
been effective in the catalytic pyrolysis and deoxygenation of oils, pro­
ducing hydrocarbons in the jet fuel range[93].
3.3. Catalyst challenges from an industrial application perspective
Although laboratory studies demonstrate that various catalysts 
exhibit outstanding performance in WCO conversion, their industrial 
application necessitates systematic evaluation of scalability, economic 
viability, and long-term robustness under real-world conditions[94]. 
Catalyst mass production involves complex engineering scaling chal­
lenges, such as ensuring uniform distribution of active components 
across macroscopic carriers, guaranteeing mechanical strength, and 
optimizing forming processes. These factors collectively determine the 
final cost and performance of the catalyst[95].
In terms of economics, while non-precious metal catalysts (such as 
Ni, Co, Mo) offer advantages in raw material costs, they typically require 
more stringent operating conditions. Additionally, issues like coking and 
sintering may necessitate more frequent regeneration or shorten service 
life, thereby impacting total lifecycle costs. Precious metal catalysts, 
though highly active, are expensive and more sensitive to impurities like 
sulfur and chlorine in feedstocks, limiting their application scenarios. 
Beyond this, the economic viability of the process also heavily depends 
on the availability and cost of external hydrogen sources. The current 
mainstream "gray hydrogen" significantly undermines the lifecycle car­
bon reduction benefits of WCO-SAF, while the use of "green hydrogen" or 
"blue hydrogen" is an inevitable choice for achieving deep decarbon­
ization. However, this also poses challenges for project siting and cost 
structure[96].Comparing the two mainstream routes, HEFA and CHJ, 
reveals significant differences in their technical and economic viability. 
The HEFA process is mature and currently dominates production ca­
pacity, but its high hydrogen consumption and stringent raw material 
quality requirements pose major constraints. The CHJ process offers 
potential advantages in feedstock adaptability (capable of processing 
high-moisture, high-acid-value materials) and hydrogen consumption, 
making it more suitable for treating low-quality waste crude oil. How­
ever, its high-pressure reaction environment imposes higher demands 
on equipment engineering and the hydrothermal stability of catalysts.
Catalyst deactivation is a core challenge in industrial operations. 
Unlike refined vegetable oils, waste cooking oils contain complex im­
purities originating from the cooking process, food residues, and storage 
contamination. These primarily include sulfur, chlorine, and phos­
phorus compounds, along with trace heavy metals[97]. Such impurities 
irreversibly poison the active sites of metals through chemical adsorp­
tion or react with the acidic centers of the carrier, permanently 
impairing the catalyst's hydrogenation and acid functions. Therefore, 
the development of a catalyst system that combines high activity with 
strong resistance to poisoning.
Carbon-based materials (activated carbon, carbon nanotubes) 
leverage their chemical inertness, hydrophobicity, and high specific 
surface area to primarily accommodate impurities through physical 
adsorption[98]. They provide effective buffering for active metals, 
demonstrating outstanding comprehensive resistance. Aluminum phos­
phate molecular sieves (e.g., SAPO-11) inherently tolerate phosphorus 
impurities due to phosphorus-containing frameworks, while their 
moderate acidity helps maintain isomerization activity and stability. In 
contrast, conventional γ-Al2O3 readily deactivates upon phosphorus 
binding, and the micropores of silica-alumina zeolites are prone to im­
purity blockage[99]. When preparing catalysts for WCO, resistance to 
poisoning must be prioritized as equally important as intrinsic activity. 
This requires combining tolerant supports (e.g., carbon materials, 
SAPOs) with robust active phases (e.g., NiMo, Pt-Sn alloys)[100]. 
Furthermore, constructing composite oxides (e.g., TiO2-ZrO2) or 
designing functionalized supports with core–shell structures and hier­
archical pore systems to synergistically build multi-layered protection at 
both physical and chemical levels represents a key direction for devel­
oping next-generation highly stable WCO treatment catalysts[101].
In summary, future R&D efforts must shift from a purely activity- 
driven approach to comprehensive performance and cost optimization 
across the entire lifecycle. Emphasis should be placed on conducting 
long-term operational testing using real, fluctuating WCO feedstock at 
pilot or industrial demonstration scales. This should be coupled with 
technical–economic analysis and life cycle assessment of the system to 
accelerate the commercialization of high-performance catalysts.
4. Influence of process conditions and optimization
The efficiency and economics of bio-aviation fuel preparation 
fundamentally depend on precise control of core operational parame­
ters. These parameters not only directly influence reaction rates and 
thermodynamic equilibrium but also profoundly regulate reaction net­
works and the intrinsic properties of catalysts through complex 
interactions.
4.1. Effect of reaction temperature and pressure
Temperature and pressure, as the most fundamental thermodynamic 
variables, must be set in close alignment with the chemical nature of the 
target reaction stage. During the initial hydrogenation and saturation 
phase of the HEFA process, the primary objective is to eliminate the 
double bonds in the glycerol triglycerides present in the feedstock. This 
exothermic reaction (ΔH < 0) is thermodynamically more favorable at 
lower temperatures (200–300◦C) and effectively suppresses side re­
actions such as C–C bond cleavage. For example, Sinopec's SRJET 
technology employs a Ni-based catalyst at 250◦C and 5 MPa to convert 
triglycerides into fatty acids via β-hydrogen elimination during the hy­
drogenation saturation stage [94]. However, low temperatures imply 
slower reaction kinetics, which must be compensated for by extending 
residence time or enhancing catalyst activity. The accompanying 
hydrogen pressure (typically 3–6 MPa) must be sufficient to ensure 
adequate hydrogen coverage on the catalyst surface, thereby achieving

high conversion rates[102].
Entering the core stage of hydrogenation deoxidation (HDO), the 
effects of temperature and pressure become more complex. Temperature 
is the primary factor determining the selectivity of the reaction pathway. 
Studies indicate that the three primary dehydrogenation pathways, 
hydrogenation (HDO), decarboxylation (DCO), and decarboxylation 
oxidation (DCOx), exhibit distinct activation energies. Moderately 
elevated temperatures (350–400◦C) significantly accelerate all path­
ways, ensuring complete conversion of feedstocks. For instance, waste 
lard achieves a 96.62% conversion rate at 400◦C [103]. However, the 
effect of temperature on selectivity is nonlinear: below a certain 
threshold (e.g., 300◦C for specific catalysts), it may predominantly 
promote the HDO pathway, yielding straight-chain alkanes matching 
the carbon number of the feedstock. Beyond this threshold, pathways 
causing carbon loss (e.g., DCO/DCOx) and secondary reactions like 
cracking and aromatization intensify[104,105]. Dynamic studies by 
Yuwei Bie et al. indicate that for HDO of fatty acid methyl esters, optimal 
equilibrium can be achieved at 300◦C and 8 MPa. Reaction temperature 
significantly influences the HDO reaction pathway of methyl esters, 
while hydrogen pressure exerts a relatively minor effect[104]. 
Compared to the dominant role of temperature, hydrogen pressure ex­
erts a relatively weaker direct influence on pathway selectivity. Its pri­
mary function lies in providing an ample hydrogen supply for the 
hydrogenation step by enhancing the solubility of liquid-phase 
hydrogen and increasing the partial pressure of hydrogen at the cata­
lyst surface. This indirectly promotes the HDO pathway and rapidly 
saturates unsaturated intermediates, thereby inhibiting their polymeri­
zation and coking. Consequently, it maintains catalyst activity and 
stability.
The temperatures in the isomerization and cracking stages are typi­
cally lower than or equal to those in the HDO stage, with a typical 
operating temperature range between 340◦C and 400◦C. Temperature is 
the key parameter for controlling cracking depth and isomerization 
selectivity. Isomerization reactions generally dominate at relatively 
lower temperatures. As temperature increases, the rate of hydrocracking 
reactions significantly rises, leading to the breakage of long-chain al­
kanes. Moderately increasing temperature can enhance the yield of jet 
fuel fractions (C8-C16). However, excessively high temperatures cause 
excessive cracking, generating large amounts of light gases (C1-C4) and 
gasoline fractions, resulting in a sharp decline in jet fuel yield. For 
example, on Pt/Beta catalysts, an increase in reaction temperature from 
300◦C to 400◦C results in a decrease in liquid product yields and an 
increase in gas and coke yields, while aromatics yields increase from 4.5 
to 15.63%[106]. The elevated hydrogen pressure at this stage sup­
presses the polymerization and coking of olefins generated by dehy­
drogenation reactions at the acidic sites of the catalyst, thereby 
protecting the catalyst and extending its operational lifespan. Simulta­
neously, the high-pressure environment facilitates rapid hydrogenation 
of unsaturated intermediates, enhancing product stability. Typical 
pressure ranges reported in the literature are 7–10 MPa.
Compared to the more mature HEFA process with distinct reaction 
steps, one-step hydrogenation and catalytic hydrothermal cracking 
processes couple more complex reaction networks within the reactor, 
making them more demanding in terms of temperature and pressure 
requirements. To achieve efficient deoxygenation, cracking, and isom­
erization simultaneously within a single reaction space, one-step pro­
cesses typically require precise balancing within a relatively narrow 
operating window[107]. The optimal deoxidation reaction parameters 
typically fall within the range of 350–400◦C and 1–5 MPa[108]. How­
ever, the specific optimal value strongly depends on the catalyst system 
and feedstock characteristics. For example, ZSM-22 molecular sieves 
have the best isomerization efficiency at 320◦C (72% of isoparaffins), 
but exceeding 350◦C leads to molecular sieve skeleton collapse 
[109,110].
In contrast, the “temperature-pressure” synergistic regulation in the 
catalytic hydrothermal decomposition (CHJ) process exhibits a two- 
stage characteristic and generally requires higher pressure. In the first 
stage of hydrothermal liquefaction (HTL), the core process involves 
converting wet biomass into bio-crude oil using subcritical water. This 
procedure typically occurs in a medium-temperature, high-pressure 
aqueous environment, with typical operating temperatures ranging from 
250–380◦C. Pressure must be maintained at elevated levels of 5–30 MPa 
to sustain water in its liquid state and facilitate reactions such as hy­
drolysis and pyrolysis[111]. Temperature is the decisive factor for bio- 
crude oil yield at this stage, with a clearly defined optimum value. For 
example, using microalgae with 78.4% water content as feedstock, a bio- 
crude oil yield of 37% can be achieved at 300◦C and 10 MPa[112]. BAO 
et al. found that at 310◦C, the bio-crude oil yield reached a peak of 
36.75%[113]. Research indicates that the energy provided by hydro­
thermal liquefaction reactions at low temperatures is insufficient, 
resulting in low conversion rates. As temperatures rise, the dissolution 
rate of organic compounds accelerates, promoting multiple reactions 
(hydrolysis, decarboxylation, aromatization, etc.), thereby accelerating 
the formation of bio-crude oil. However, excessively high temperatures 
cause intermediate products in this process to readily form coke and 
increase gas yield[114]. The subsequent bio-crude hydrogenation 
upgrading stage operates on a similar principle to HEFA hydrogenation. 
However, since its feedstock (bio-crude) typically contains a higher 
proportion of unsaturated bonds and aromatic structures, it often re­
quires higher hydrogen partial pressures (e.g., 10–20 MPa) to promote 
deep hydrogenation saturation and suppress condensation coking re­
actions. Consequently, the overall pressure requirements of the CHJ 
process are significantly higher than those of HEFA and one-step pro­
cesses using oils as feedstock.
The effects of temperature and pressure are not isolated but act 
synergistically on reaction equilibrium and rate. When increasing tem­
perature to pursue high conversion rates, hydrogen pressure must be 
raised simultaneously to suppress the heightened risk of coking and 
deactivation. The synergistic requirements for temperature and pressure 
in different Bio-jet fuel preparation processes fundamentally reflect the 
characteristics of the reactants, differences in catalysts (such as the 
varying hydrogenolysis activities of noble metals versus transition 
metals), and the degree of coupling with the target reaction pathway. 
These parameters must be jointly determined based on the interplay 
between "catalyst properties, feedstock characteristics, and target 
product distribution"[115].
4.2. Effect of Hydrogen-to-Oil ratio
The impact of the hydrogen-to-oil ratio (defined as the ratio of 
hydrogen gas volume to feedstock oil volume under standard conditions, 
H2/oil) is multidimensional. The impact of hydrogen-to-oil ratio on the 
hydrogenation esterification and fatty acid (HEFA) process is primarily 
reflected in three aspects: reaction pathways, product selectivity, and 
catalyst lifetime. A higher hydrogen-to-oil ratio implies a higher 
hydrogen partial pressure, which promotes the hydrogen-consuming 
HDO pathway, enhances the selectivity of straight-chain alkanes, and 
effectively delays catalyst deactivation by carbon deposits through 
surface flushing[103]. Conversely, low hydrogen oil ratios (e.g., <500 
Nm3/m3) restrict hydrogen surface coverage. This not only shifts the 
reaction toward low-hydrogen-consumption DCO/DCOx pathways, 
leading to reduced jet fuel selectivity (potentially below 35%), but also 
accelerates catalyst deactivation due to intermediate polymerization 
[116,117].
Research indicates that under startup conditions characterized by 
low oil-to-hydrogen ratios and low hydrogen pressure, carbonaceous 
deposits permanently adsorb onto active sites, leading to severe and 
irreversible catalyst deactivation[117]. Therefore, during the hydroge­
nation saturation and HDO stages, a higher hydrogen-to-oil ratio (typi­
cally recommended at 500–1200 Nm3/m3) is essential to ensure reaction 
depth and catalyst stability. However, in the isomerization stage, 
excessively high hydrogen partial pressure may overly suppress the

dehydrogenation step at metal sites (a prerequisite for generating olefin 
intermediates for subsequent acid-catalyzed isomerization). Thus, the 
hydrogen-to-oil ratio must be moderately reduced in this stage (e.g., 
300–1000 Nm3/m3) to maintain the delicate equilibrium between metal 
sites (dehydrogenation/hydrogenation) and acidic sites (isomerization) 
within the bifunctional catalyst[107].
The one-step process also relies on external hydrogen, but its opti­
mization objectives are more complex: excessively high hydrogen par­
tial pressure inhibits the dehydrogenation step that generates olefin 
intermediates at metal sites, a prerequisite for subsequent acid-catalyzed 
isomerization. Therefore, the one-step process must balance promoting 
hydrogenation-deoxygenation with maintaining essential dehydroge­
nation activity. For example, when using SDBS-Pt/SAPO-11 to catalyze 
the oxidation of castor oil (Jatropha oil), the activity is approximately 
1000 Nm3/m3[118]. For the CHJ process, the hydrogen concept in its 
first stage has been fundamentally redefined. The reaction relies on in- 
situ hydrogen production via aqueous-phase reforming, making the 
water-to-oil ratio—rather than the hydrogen-to-oil ratio—the core 
parameter. The water-to-oil ratio not only influences the physicochem­
ical properties of the reaction medium but also directly correlates with 
the in-situ hydrogen production rate. In the subsequent bio-crude hy­
drogenation upgrading stage, although external hydrogen supplemen­
tation is required, the objective is to achieve deep saturation of highly 
unsaturated complex molecules. Consequently, the required hydrogen 
partial pressure is typically far higher than in the HEFA process 
(reaching 10–20 MPa).
The hydrogen-oil ratio fundamentally represents the macroscopic 
manifestation of hydrogen chemical potential within the reaction sys­
tem. A high hydrogen-oil ratio (high hydrogen partial pressure) un­
equivocally benefits all hydrogenation steps by promoting the HDO 
pathway, enhancing alkane selectivity, and effectively delaying catalyst 
deactivation due to coking. However, a delicate trade-off exists during 
the isomerization stage: excessively high hydrogen partial pressure in­
hibits the formation of olefin intermediates on metal active sites, which 
are essential precursors for acidic sites to perform skeletal isomerization. 
Therefore, the industrial practice often employs a staged hydrogen-to-oil 
ratio adjustment strategy: a high hydrogen-to-oil ratio is maintained in 
the main hydrogenation-deoxygenation section to ensure thorough 
deoxygenation and catalyst protection; while the hydrogen-to-oil ratio is 
moderately reduced in the isomerization section to optimize isomeri­
zation efficiency[119].
4.3. Effect of liquid hourly space velocity and reaction time
Both liquid hourly space velocity (typically expressed as liquid 
hourly space velocity, LHSV, defined as the volume of liquid feedstock 
processed per unit time per unit volume of catalyst) and reaction time 
have significant effects on the HEFA and CHJ, two processes for the 
production of Bio-jet fuel, but there are differences in the mechanism of 
the effects and the direction of optimization.
For the HEFA processes, the reaction time directly affects the depth 
of hydrocracking and product distribution. For example, at 350◦C, the 
yield of Bio-jet fuel increases significantly from about 15% to 38% as the 
reaction time increases from 10 to 30 min[120]. The LHSV is a very 
critical parameter in the HEFA process, which determines the residence 
time of the feedstock in the catalyst bed and directly affects the degree of 
completion of the reactions such as hydrogenation, deoxygenation, 
isomerization and cracking[121]. Excessively high liquid hourly space 
velocity can result in insufficient contact time and incomplete conver­
sion. A lower liquid hourly space velocity (i.e., longer residence time) 
typically facilitates adequate contact between reactants and the catalyst, 
promoting the reaction and enhancing the selectivity of the target 
product. However, excessively long residence times may cause over- 
cracking, ultimately producing excessive light hydrocarbons (such as 
naphtha), reducing the selectivity of the jet fuel fraction (C8-C16), and 
adversely affecting catalyst lifespan. In industrial production, different 
liquid hourly space velocity and reaction times are typically set in stages. 
During the heavy oil desulfurization stage, the LHSV ranges from 1.0 to 
1.5 h−1 (reaction time 10–60 min). For example, at a zero-rate LHSV of 
1.0 h−1 on the Pt/Al-MCM-41 catalyst, the coal vapor ratio (aerosol/ 
light hydrocarbons) reaches its peak. During the isomerization stage, 
LHSV ranges from 1.0 to 2.5 h−1 (reaction time 24–30 min). For 
instance, at 2.5 h−1, the Pt/modified USY catalyst achieves jet fuel pour 
points below −50◦C[84,122].
For the CHJ process, the reaction time in the first stage is an inde­
pendent critical variable that requires precise control to balance the 
thorough depolymerization of biomacromolecules with the secondary 
repolymerization/coking of intermediate products. Insufficient reten­
tion time prevents the complete conversion of high-molecular-weight 
organic compounds in the waste into bio-crude oil, thereby reducing 
bio-crude oil yield. As the reaction time increases, bio-crude oil pro­
duction significantly improves. Conversely, an excessively long reaction 
time causes intermediate products to undergo repolymerization, form­
ing coke, or undergo gasification, producing CO2, H2, and other gases 
[123,124]. Research indicates that most liquefaction reactions can be 
completed within a relatively short period of time (e.g., 15–60 min) 
[111]. For example, a study determined that three hours was the optimal 
reaction time when converting palm oil into jet fuel fractions at 400◦C 
[125]. Additionally, the effects of reaction time and temperature can be 
comprehensively evaluated using the reaction intensity factor (KSF). 
The highest biofuel conversion rate occurs when the KSF value ranges 
between 9 and 10[126]. In the second-stage hydrogenation upgrading 
process, to ensure complete reaction of complex components, removal of 
heteroatoms, and attainment of the required degree of saturation, a 
space-velocity range of 0.1 h−1 to 0.8 h−1 is typically employed for 
upgrading bio-crude oil[127].
Liquid hourly space velocity (or reaction time) is a key engineering 
parameter controlling reaction depth and chain reaction selectivity. 
Selecting an appropriate range within the process ensures sufficient 
contact between reactant molecules and active sites of the catalyst 
without inducing secondary cracking[128]. For the hydrothermal 
liquefaction stage of the CHJ process, an optimal reaction time window 
exists to balance the complete liquefaction of macromolecules with the 
undesirable repolymerization of intermediates. The core challenge in 
industrial design lies in maximizing the reactor volumetric efficiency by 
optimizing catalyst activity to achieve the highest possible liquid hourly 
space velocity, while maintaining the selectivity of the target products.
5. Conclusions and Outlook
5.1. Conclude
The conversion of WCO into SAF represents a technologically viable 
and strategically significant pathway for decarbonizing the aviation 
sector. This review has systematically examined the two most prominent 
technological routes, HEFA and CHJ, underscoring that catalyst design 
and process optimization are the central pillars determining efficiency, 
product quality, and economic feasibility. The mature and robust HEFA 
process relies on an advanced bifunctional catalyst system, while the 
emerging CHJ process offers greater feedstock flexibility and lower 
hydrogen consumption. However, it imposes stricter requirements on 
catalyst hydrothermal stability and process engineering.
A critical synthesis of the literature reveals that the evolution of 
catalyst systems, from conventional sulfide catalysts to advanced 
bimetallic, bifunctional, and molecular sieve-based designs—has been 
primarily driven by the need to enhance activity, selectivity, and 
robustness against the complex impurities inherent in WCO. The per­
formance of these catalysts is inextricably linked to precise process 
conditions, including temperature, pressure, hydrogen-to-oil ratio, and 
liquid hourly space velocity, which collectively dictate reaction path­
ways, product distribution, and catalyst longevity. Despite significant 
progress, the transition from laboratory-scale excellence to robust, cost-

5.2. Challenges and prospects for the future
5.2.1. Technical challenges and responses
The pathway to large-scale commercialization of WCO-SAF faces 
persistent technical hurdles that demand focused innovation. Catalyst 
durability remains a primary concern. The complex impurity profile of 
WCO, including sulfur, chlorine, phosphorus, and trace metals, leads to 
irreversible poisoning and fouling of active sites. The current phys­
ical–chemical deacidification process has high energy consumption, 
while novel enzymatic deacidification (e.g., lipase-catalyzed ethanol­
ysis) can reduce FFA to 0.25% under mild conditions at 40◦C, but the 
large-scale application is still limited by enzyme cost and stability[43]. 
Furthermore, the high-temperature, high-pressure, and aqueous envi­
ronments involved, particularly in CHJ, accelerate sintering, leaching, 
and support collapse. Future catalyst development must prioritize the 
design of "smart" materials with built-in resistance. This involves engi­
neering robust supports (e.g., carbon-based materials, doped composite 
oxides), tailoring active phase compositions (e.g., forming stable alloys 
or intermetallic compounds), and constructing protective architectures 
like core–shell structures to physically and chemically shield active sites.
Process integration and energy efficiency present another major 
challenge. The HEFA process is energy- and hydrogen-intensive, with its 
carbon footprint heavily dependent on the source of hydrogen. The CHJ 
process, while less hydrogen-intensive, requires significant energy for 
maintaining high-pressure conditions. Responses should focus on 
developing low-severity catalytic processes, optimizing heat integration 
networks, and coupling SAF production with renewable hydrogen 
sources (green hydrogen) or in-situ hydrogen generation technologies to 
achieve true lifecycle carbon neutrality. Moreover, the development of 
robust, cost-effective pretreatment technologies to standardize highly 
variable WCO feedstocks is crucial for ensuring consistent catalyst per­
formance and final fuel quality.
5.2.2. Prospects
Despite many challenges, technological innovation and policy 
drivers are accelerating the commercialization of SAF from WCO. In 
terms of catalyst stability enhancement, future research should be 
devoted to the development of multi-functional catalysts with low cost, 
high stability and anti-poisoning, especially non-precious metal cata­
lysts and molecular sieve materials with graded pores. In terms of pro­
cess, processes such as one-step hydrogenation and catalytic 
hydrothermal cracking need to be further optimized to reduce energy 
and hydrogen consumption, and focus on green-hydrogen coupling and 
reaction integration to enhance sustainability. Standardized pretreat­
ment of feedstock is critical to ensure feed quality and process stability. 
Promoting the construction of pilot and demonstration plants with 
comprehensive techno-economic and full life-cycle assessments is key to 
validating their commercialization feasibility and gaining policy 
support.
Furthermore, the integration of WCO-SAF production with other 
waste valorization streams (e.g., biogas upgrading, municipal solid 
waste processing) within a biorefinery concept can enhance overall 
economics and sustainability. From a policy and market perspective, the 
strengthening of mandates like ReFuelEU and the development of robust 
carbon accounting and trading mechanisms will provide stable demand 
signals, accelerating investment and scale-up. As technological readi­
ness advances and costs decline, WCO-SAF is poised to transition from a 
niche supplement to a mainstream component of the global aviation fuel 
mix, playing an indispensable role in the sector's journey toward net- 
zero emissions.
