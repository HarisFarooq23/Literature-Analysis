A B S T R A C T
Developing sustainable aviation fuel (SAF) is essential for achieving net-zero emissions, yet economic viability 
remains a major challenge. In this study, the catalytic deoxygenation (DO) of waste cooking oil was investigated 
using nickel promoted catalyst such as Ni/V2O5 and Ni/AC, which were synthesized via wet-impregnation using 
treatments of microwave irradiation (M), sonication (S) and conventional stirring (C). Physico-chemical prop­
erties revealed an M method showed improved surface area, high pore volume, enhanced nickel dispersion, 
optimized acidity than S and C method, suggesting M method induced the formation of metallic phase through 
fast, volumetric heating that accelerated reduction, nucleation and crystallization. Similarly, with DO activity, 
Ni/V2O5 M showed comparable or superior performance to C and S method at 95% reduced time, 68% less 
energy consumption with 96% hydrocarbon liquid yield and 78% jet-fraction selectivity under H2-free condition 
at 400 ℃ for 2 h over 5 wt% loading. Enhanced performance is attributed to the strong metal-support interaction, 
where Ni-O-V interfacial sites generated rich oxygen vacancies that promote DO while Ni0 facilitated

1. Introduction
The aviation industry faces an urgent need for sustainable fuels to 
mitigate climate change. The negative environmental impacts of fossil- 
based fuels, coupled with their rapid depletion and volatile market 
prices, have intensified global efforts to develop clean and renewable 
energy alternatives. The aviation sector faces growing challenges as air 
travel demand continues to rise, leading to increasing CO2 emissions 
that significantly contribute to climate change (Adzahar et al., 2026). 
Bio-jet fuel (BJF), composed primarily of C8-C16 hydrocarbons, has 
emerged as a promising sustainable alternative. BJF cuts life-cycle 
greenhouse gases while maintaining drop-in compatibility, in addition 
to lower sulfur content, lower tailpipe emissions, high thermal stability, 
and favourable cold-flow properties (Hebish et al., 2024). Triglyceride 
oils represent attractive feedstocks for BJF production, as their long- 
chain fatty acids closely resemble hydrocarbons. However, upgrading 
and oxygen removal are required, typically achieved through deoxy­
genation (DO) and hydrodeoxygenation (HDO). HDO relies on sub­
stantial amounts of high-pressure hydrogen gas, which raises safety 
concerns over storage, delivery and operational hazards and undermines 
sustainability, since clean H2 production is far from commercialization 
and thus approximately 96% of global hydrogen is currently derived 
from non-renewable sources such as naphtha natural gas reforming. In 
contrast, DO does not require external H2 input; instead, oxygen is 
removed through decarboxylation (CO2 release) or decarbonylation (CO 
and H2O release) collectively referred to as DeCox pathways, offering a 
more sustainable H2-free pathway for BJF production. Note, CO and CO2 
gases by-products of DO reaction can be captured and not released into 
the environment. On an industrial or pilot scale with a complete system, 
these gases will be recycled back and used as feedstocks to produce other 
valuable chemicals and intermediates such as methane, urea, syngas etc 
(James et al., 2023; Zhang et al., 2020). Several studies have reported on 
the promising potential of DO reactions. For instance, Why et al., 2022
reported a 96% liquid yield and 73% jet fuel selectivity for the DO of 
palm kernel oil under Pd/C. In another study, Asikin-Mijan et al., 2022
observed 77% hydrocarbon yield and 64% jet fuel selectivity for the DO 
of tung oil under CoW/SiO2-Al2O3. Furthermore, the DO of soybean oil 
under NbOPO4 was found to yield 91% liquid product and 58% jet-range 
selectivity (Scaldaferri and Pasa, 2019). Despite these advances, catalyst 
efficiency and energy consumption remain critical challenges.
Waste Cooking Oil (WCO) is a major byproduct of food preparation, 
and improper disposal poses serious environmental hazards. Globally, 
around 16.5 million tonnes of WCO are generated annually, creating 
challenges for environmentally safe disposal (Sharma et al., 2021). 
Palm-based WCO, comprising mainly of palmitic acid (C16:0) and oleic 
acid (C18:1), presents an attractive feedstock to produce BJF. In fact, 
WCO can produce highly competitive fuel and potentially most cost- 
effective, along with avoiding disposal hazards and avoiding direct 
competition with edible oil feedstocks in the food supply chain (Detsios 
et al., 2023).
Although noble metal catalysts exhibit excellent DO performance, 
their prohibitive cost and scarcity limit large-scale application (Hebish 
et al., 2024). Similarly, sulfided catalysts, despite their effectiveness, 
face challenges such as sulfur leaching and contamination of the final 
product, making them less desirable as catalysts for biofuel synthesis 
(Gamal et al., 2020). Consequently, this has driven the search for 
affordable, sulfur-free alternatives. In this regard, transition metals and 
their oxides have emerged as promising alternatives. Among them, Ni- 
based catalysts offer a sustainable and industrial relevant alternative 
in upgrading of triglyceride oils for biofuel production owing to their 
relatively low cost and scalability and demonstrated strong 
hydrogenation-DO capability and high catalytic activity (Aliana- 
Nasharuddin et al., 2020; Hafriz et al., 2022; Hartono et al., 2023; Khalit 
et al., 2021). As reported by Morgan et al., Ni showed higher DO and 
cracking activity than Pd/Pt on similar supports, yielding more C8-C17 
hydrocarbons (Morgan et al., 2010). Similar behaviour was observed by 
Jeon et al. during the DO of oleic acid over a Ni-CeZrO2 catalyst, which 
attained ~ 98% conversion C9-C18 hydrocarbon fraction of 34% and an 
oxygen removal efficiency of ~ 96% (Jeon et al., 2019). Furthermore, in 
a comparative study conducted by Hafriz and colleagues, transition 
metals Ni, Zn, Cu, Co and Fe were evaluated as catalyst promoters. 
Among these catalysts, findings reveal the Ni-based catalyst system 
exhibited the highest conversion activity (67%), high portion of satu­
rated hydrocarbons (80%), and highest selectivity towards green diesel 
(55%) and BJF (39%) (Hafriz et al., 2020). Collectively, these studies 
clearly demonstrate that Ni-promoted catalysts enhance DO activity. 
Among Ni-based catalyst, Ni supported on activated carbon (AC) shows 
great potential. Previously, Wu et al. performed DO on stearic acid over 
Ni/AC achieving an almost complete conversion with heptadecane (C17) 
selectivity of ~ 80% (Wu et al., 2016). The research group of (Khalit 
et al., 2021) tested Ni catalyst over various supports of AC, zeolite, 
and reduced graphene oxide for the DO of WCO to produce green diesel. 
In their work, Ni/AC was reported the most active catalyst by giving 
90% hydrocarbon yield and 89% selectivity towards n-(C15 + C17) owing 
to its good physicochemical properties of porosity, high surface area and 
high number of acid-base sites. It is noteworthy that, vanadium pent­
oxide (V2O5) is widely recognized as a cost-effective, thermally stable 
catalytic material with excellent redox and oxygen transfer properties 
that has great value in the industry (Marberger et al., 2019). Despite this, 
as far as we know the utilization of V2O5 support remains unexplored in 
DO reaction.
Recent studies show promising results in microwave irradiation (M) 
and sonication (S)-assisted synthesis techniques of active catalysts for 
biofuel upgrading, whereby these treatments resulted in enhanced 
physicochemical properties and catalytic performance (Luo et al., 2022). 
M method induces volumetric heating that leads to uniform temperature 
distribution, accelerated nucleation and improved metal dispersion on 
support. Furthermore, atoms movement occurs where angles of atoms 
will shift and open up the active sites. It is also reported that catalyst 
exhibited more porosity and higher surface area following microwave 
exposure (Burakova et al., 2018). For instance, Xue et al. reported the 
development of Ni2P/SiO2 catalysts using M method and the result 
shows enhanced dispersion of Ni2P particles on support and catalytic 
performance in the HDO of methyl palmitate (Xue et al., 2015). Simi­
larly, ultrasonic approaches exploit acoustic cavitation to induce local­
ised hotspots and micro-mixing resulting in enhanced catalyst surface 
properties and metal dispersion and thus improve catalytic activity (Li 
et al., 2021). In a study conducted by (Ameen et al., 2019) Ni-Mo/ 
γ-Al2O3 catalyst synthesis by ultrasound achieved significantly higher 
hydrocarbon yields (80.87%) during the HDO of rubber seed oil 
compared to conventional prepared catalyst (63.3%).
To the best of the authors’ knowledge, no systematic study has 
directly compared M and S treatments of the same catalyst formulation 
for triglyceride DO with focus on jet-fuel range hydrocarbons. Further­
more, although Ni-based catalysts have been extensively studied and 
V2O5 exhibits excellent potential as a catalyst support, the Ni/V2O5 
bimetallic catalyst remains largely unexplored, despite its potential 
synergistic effects that could significantly enhance catalytic activity.

2. Materials and methods
2.1. Materials
Commercial Vanadium Oxide (V2O5) with purity > 99.6% was pur­
chased from Sigma-Aldrich, USA. Nickel (II) Nitrate Hexahydrate (Ni 
(NO3)2⋅6H2O) with 99% purity, Activated Carbon (AC) powder was 
supplied by Chemiz (M) Sdn. Bhd, Malaysia and Sulfuric Acid (98% 
purity) was procured from J.T. Baker, USA and n-Hexane GC grade 
procured from Supelco, Germany. Waste cooking oil (WCO) derived 
from palm oil feedstocks was obtained from a local street vendor in 
Kuala Lumpur, Malaysia. The oil obtained was left to rest for 48 h before 
carefully decanting to leave behind large impurity solids. Whatman 
grade 1 (11 μm) filter paper was used to filter finer impurities. Pre­
heating at 110 ℃ for 2 h was necessary to remove water content. The 
analysis of fatty acid composition of WCO is exhibited in Table 1. The 
acid value and free fatty acid (FFA) were quantified, and the results were 
consistent with the typical range reported in the literature (Plata et al., 
2022).
Catalysts Synthesis and Treatments.
Ni/V2O5 was prepared using the wet-impregnation method where a 
1:6.5 solid-to-liquid ratio was employed based on the support water 
solubility of 515 g/l according to Sigma Aldrich (USA). A nickel loading 
of 20 wt% was selected based on prior studies, whereby this loading was 
associated with high density of accessible Ni sites and high catalytic rate 
without excessive sintering or pore blockage (Jeon et al., 2022). 
Therefore, 9.92 g of Ni(NO3)2⋅6H2O was dissolved in 15.53 ml of 
deionised water to produce Ni salt (20 wt%) which was added dropwise 
into 8 g of V2O5 and mixed continuously at room temperature for 
approximately 12 h impregnation time, this was denoted as Ni/V2O5 
Conventional (C). In another instance the Ni salt (20 wt%) was incor­
porated into the V2O5 and exposed to 80 W microwave irradiation at 
210 rpm stirring speed for 30 min with 5 s on and 25 s off cycles using a 
household microwave oven (Sharp, Japan) and left to rest for 1 h, this 
was marked as Ni/V2O5 Microwave (M). Another case, the impregnated 
sample was subjected to sonication performed at 60% amplitude (500 
W, 20 kHz) for 30 min, utilizing a pulsed mode with 5 s on and 25 s off 
cycles and left to rest for 1 h, this was represented as Ni/V2O5 Sonicator 
(S). Similar procedures were carried out for Ni/AC C, M and S, respec­
tively. However, prior to use, the commercial activated carbon under­
went chemical activation by reflux with sulfuric acid at 150 ◦C under a 
nitrogen atmosphere for 12 h. The sample was subsequently washed 
with warm distilled water (>85 ℃) until a neutral pH (pH = 7) was 
achieved, ensuring the removal of residual sulfate ions and other un­
wanted impurities. Finally, it was subjected to drying in an oven at 100 
℃ for 12 h. All impregnated samples were dried at 100 ℃ for 24 h. 
Additionally, the catalysts underwent a necessary pre-treatment step of 
calcination where V2O5 was calcined at 600 ℃ for 4 h in air, while Ni/ 
AC and Ni/V2O5 were calcined at 600 ℃ for 4 h in a N2 inert environ­
ment, to boost their activity for the impending DO process. For com­
parison, the estimated energy consumption of each synthesis route is 
summarized in Table 2. It is worth noting that M and S methods required 
approximately 95% less time compared to conventional stirring, while 
also consuming significantly lower energy, with reductions of about 
67–77%.
2.2. Catalysts characterization
The specific surface area, pore volume, and pore size distribution of 
the samples were determined using nitrogen adsorption–desorption 
isotherms measured at liquid nitrogen temperature (−196 ◦C). The 
analysis was performed using a Micromeritics TriStar II PLUS. Prior to 
analysis, the samples were degassed under vacuum at 150 ◦C for 4 h, to 
remove any impurity gas or vapours which may have adsorbed onto the 
surface from the ambient air. The surface area was calculated using the 
Brunauer–Emmett–Teller (BET) method, while the pore size distribution 
was obtained using the Barrett–Joyner–Halenda (BJH) method. The 
crystalline phases and structural properties of the catalysts were ana­
lysed using powder X-ray diffraction (XRD). The measurements were 
carried out on a Bruker D8 Advance equipped with Cu Kα radiation (λ =
1.54060 Å), operated at 40.0 kV and 40.0 mA. Scans were conducted 
over a 2θ range of 5◦- 80◦at a scanning rate of 0.0384◦/min. The 
resulting diffractograms were compared with standard reference pat­
terns from the International Centre for Diffraction Data (ICDD) to 
identify crystalline phases, and crystallite size was calculated using 
Debye-Scherrer equation. Thermal stability of the samples was exam­
ined using thermogravimetric analysis (TGA). The analysis was per­
formed using a Mettler Toledo TGA/SDTA 851e. Approximately 10 mg 
of sample was placed in a ceramic crucible and heated 25 ◦C to 900 ◦C at 
a heating rate of 30 ◦C/min under a N2 flow of 40 mL/min. The acidity of 
the catalyst was evaluated using temperature programmed desorption 
with ammonia (TPD-NH3) analysis on a Micromeritics AutoChem II Plus.

50 ◦C and exposed to a 5% NH3/He gas mixture for 30 min. After

meritics AutoChem II Plus. The sample was first pretreated in N2 flow of

2.3. Catalytic DO reaction
The experimental setup for the deoxygenation (DO) process, as 
illustrated in Fig. S1, comprised a 500 mL triple-necked semi-batch 
reactor coupled with a condensing system. Initially, the non-pressurized 
reactor was purged with N2 to establish an inert environment by elim­
inating all O2. Subsequently, 15 g of WCO and 5 wt% catalyst were 
introduced into the reactor. The reaction mixture was then subjected to 
controlled heating and continuous stirring at 400 ℃ and 300 rpm using a 
heating mantle and stirrer equipped with a PID temperature controller, 
while maintaining a steady N2 gas flow of 20 mL/min for 2 h in atmo­
spheric pressure. Throughout the reaction, vaporized products were 
condensed via a cooling chamber maintained at 20 ◦C, with the resulting 
liquid deoxygenated product collected in a small vessel, weighed and 
subsequently analysed by gas chromatography with a flame ionization 
detector (GC-FID), gas chromatography-mass spectrometry (GC–MS) 
and Fourier transform infrared spectrometry (FTIR). The gaseous 
products were collected after 1 h reaction time and further analysed by 
gas chromatography with thermal conductivity detector (GC-TCD). To 
ensure statistical reliability, the DO process was replicated thrice, and 
the average result was calculated.
2.4. Liquid product analysis
The hydrocarbon yield was evaluated by GC-FID using an AGILENT 
7890A coupled with a HP-5 capillary column (0.25 µm x 0.32 mm x 30 
m) and utilising alkane and alkene (C8-C20) standard acquired from 
Sigma Aldrich (USA). Prior to analysis, the deoxygenated liquid product 
was diluted with GC-grade n-hexane, and ~ 2 µL of sample was injected 
into the GC system equipped with an FID detector. Helium was used as 
the carrier gas. The injector and detector temperatures were set at 250 
℃ and 300 ℃, respectively. The oven temperature was initially held at 
40 ℃ for 6 min, then ramped to 270 ℃ at a rate of 7 ℃/min. Each 
sample was analysed in triplicate to ensure reproducibility The con­
centration of various hydrocarbons (jet-range hydrocarbons: n-paraffin 
and olefin, naphthene, aromatic and heavy hydrocarbon (C > 16)) and 
byproducts or others (e.g. ketone, alcohol, and carboxylic acid) were 
identified with GC–MS, using SHIMADZU GC-TQ8030 equipped with a 
SH-5MS capillary column (thickness: 0.25 µm x length: 30 m x diameter: 
0.25 mm), with analysis carried out under similar operating conditions 
to GC-FID. Compounds were identified from the GC–MS spectra using 
the National Institute of Standards and Technology (NIST) library based 
on a probability match of 95% or higher. Fourier transform infrared 
(FTIR) spectroscopy was performed using a Shimadzu IRAffinity-1S 
spectrophotometer. Spectra were recorded in the range of 650–4000 
cm−1 to identify the chemical functional groups present in the liquid 
product. Gas products collected were analysed by the Agilent USA model 
GC6890 N equipped with a TCD via an offline sampling method to 
determine the composition and concentration of the gases produced 
during reaction. The mass yield, hydrocarbon yield and jet-selectivity

3. Results and discussion
3.1. Characterization of synthesised catalysts
XRD patterns (Fig. S2a-b) confirm orthorhombic V2O5 (JCPDS card 
no 01–075-9978), and nickel vanadate phases such as Ni3(VO4), Ni 
(V2O6) and Ni2(V2O7) in all Ni/V2O5 catalysts. The absence of distinct 
metallic Ni or NiO indicates the formation of strong Nickel Vanadium 
Oxide interactions during synthesis and calcination, producing mixed 
oxide phases instead of separate Ni or NiO domains, this is in line with 
previous reports where incorporation of Ni into oxide supports sup­
presses Ni and NiO reflections in XRD (Adzahar et al., 2026). In the case 
of Ni/AC catalysts result reveals sharp reflections indexed to metallic Ni 
(FCC, JCPDS card no. 00–004-0850) implying a greater proportion of 
crystalline Ni0 sites along with the broad band of graphitic carbon at 
about 2θ = 26◦. Trace NiO (JCPDS card no. 01–078-0643) is only 
detected in the C sample, whereas both M and S exhibit exclusively 
metallic Ni, implying that M and S treatments favour full reduction of Ni 
species during synthesis likely due to the rapid heating and accelerated 
nucleation of M and S-assistance. This observation was previously 
confirmed by Lim et al., 2021 from using H2-TPR where they reported 
that sonicated catalysts display more reducible NiO, indicating a higher 
fraction of Ni can be reduced to metallic Ni (Ni0). A similar outcome for 
microwaved catalysts was revealed by (Aimdate et al., 2021). Further­
more, the crystallite size was determined at the most intense peak at 2θ 
= 44.5◦(Ni) and 2θ = 20.4◦(V2O5) for Ni/AC and Ni/V2O5 catalysts, 
respectively (Table 3). In terms of Ni/AC catalysts, crystallite size fol­
lowed the decreasing trend S > C > M, suggesting that M method 
improved Ni dispersion and availability of active sites. Surprisingly, the 
crystallite size of Ni on Ni/AC obtained from S method was the largest 
(24 nm) among the rest AC supported catalyst. Former literature reports 
that for carbon-based supports dispersion quality depends on sonication 
energy density, too low energy leaves large agglomerates and too high 
causes re-agglomeration, thereby increasing crystallite size (Rennhofer 
and Zanghellini, 2021). For Ni/V2O5 catalyst crystallite size decreased 
in the order C > S > M, revealing greater dispersion by M system. Note, C 
method for both catalyst series showed to favour the formation of large 
crystallite size and reduced dispersion. Remarkably, the notable 
decrease in crystallinity for Ni/V2O5 M suggests greater dispersion 
resulting in finer crystallites and greater lattice imperfections, which can 
be attributed to the rapid disorderly atom arrangement due to micro­
wave volumetric heating, therefore promoting the generation of more 
interfacial Ni-O-V sites which are reported to exhibit Ni2+/Ni3+ and 
V5+/V4+ redox couples with lattice oxygen participation and likely 
originated from Ni3(VO4), Ni(V2O6) and Ni2(V2O7) phases (Li et al., 
2020). Such redox-active sites are known to promote C-–O bond 
cleavage and oxygen removal facilitating DeCOx pathways (Song et al., 
2023).
BET analysis (Table 3, Fig. S2c-f) shows synthesis methods strongly 
influence surface area and porosity. As anticipated, the Ni/AC catalyst 
series exhibited the highest surface area, owing to the inherent porous 
structure of carbon. The BET surface area revealed was ~ 0.71–––2.56

The H2-TPR profiles of V2O5 and Ni/V2O5 catalyst series, shown in 
Fig. S3(a), reveal that the incorporation of Ni significantly enhances the 
reducibility of V2O5. While pure V2O5 exhibits high-temperature 
reduction peaks (~580-830℃), Ni/V2O5 catalysts display prominent 
low-temperature peaks (~490-580℃) indicating the presence of more 
easily reducible oxygen species. This shift to lower temperature suggests 
that Ni addition facilitates the formation of oxygen vacancies (Ov), 
particularly at the Ni-O-V interface sites. The higher peak area of Ni/ 
V2O5 M and therefore the higher total H2 consumed implies greater 
concentration of reactive Ov, providing more active site for enhanced 
DO. The rapid microwave heating promotes accelerated nucleation 
leading to lattice imperfections that generate Ni-O-V interface sites with 
rich Ov.
The H2-TPR profiles of Ni/AC catalyst series is depicted in Fig. S3(b). 
Indeed, all catalysts exhibited a single peak with varying intensity and 
broadness. Ni/AC C exhibited a broad and intense reduction peak cen­
tred at ~ 285℃ which indicates the presence of relatively high amount 
of reducible NiO species alongside Ni. This observation corroborates the 
XRD results, where NiO species in C method were present above the 
detection limit and therefore identified. Interestingly, the M and S 
methods display substantially lower hydrogen consumption with 
reduction peaks centred at 220℃ and 250℃, respectively. The weaker 
peak intensity and lower temperature are attributed to the presence of 
minimal NiO species that have formed a thin layer on the surface due to 
partial oxidation of metallic Ni upon exposure to air. Thus, the nickel 
phase is predominantly in the metallic state. This result was also in 
accordance with XRD findings, where trace NiO was below detection 
limit and therefore not identified.
Catalyst acidity is crucial for facilitating the cleavage of both C-–O 
and C––C bonds during the DO reaction (Asikin-Mijan et al., 2017). 
Therefore, acidity characteristics of the synthesized catalyst were eval­
uated by TPD-NH3, and the profiles for the Ni/V2O5 and Ni/AC catalyst 
series are presented in Fig. S3(c) and (d), respectively, and total acid 
sites are summarized in Table 3. From the outset, the acidity profiles 
show significant variations influenced by support type and treatment 
method. All the catalysts displayed three principal desorption regions 
corresponding to weak acid sites (< 250 ℃), medium acid sites (250 ℃ 
−
500 ℃) and strong acid sites (> 500 ℃). The total acid sites of the 
catalysts are in the increasing order, Ni/V2O5 C < Ni/V2O5 S < V2O5 <
Ni/V2O5 M < Ni/AC M < Ni/AC S < Ni/AC C. Indeed, the acid rich 
species of activated carbon is attributed to the presence of surface ox­
ygen functional groups, including lactonic, carboxylic, and phenolic 
groups (Khalit et al., 2021). These organic groups are ascribed to the 
acidification of AC via concentrated acid, this observation is in line with 
the findings of Asikin-Mijan et al. and Gamal et al. (Asikin-Mijan et al., 
2017; Gamal et al., 2020). It is important to note that an excessive 
density of acidic sites can promote undesirable side reactions, leading to 
accelerated coke formation and consequently severe catalyst deactiva­
tion (Aliana-Nasharuddin et al., 2020; Khalit et al., 2021). Thus, higher 
acidity in Ni/AC catalysts may enhance DO activity but risks coke for­
mation. From the TPD-NH3 graph (Fig. S4), original V2O5 is acidic which 
can be attributed to NH3 desorbed from Lewis sites (Tmax ≈150 ℃), 
weak Brønsted sites (Tmax ≈410 ℃) and strong Brønsted sites (Tmax ≈
680 ℃) (Mulyatun et al., 2024). The Brønsted sites are mainly associated 
with V4+–OH groups, while Lewis acidity originates from exposed V5+
centres capable of accepting electron pairs (Zhao et al., 2018). It is 
observed that the integration of Ni into V2O5 and the treatment process 
influenced the degree of Ni dispersion (see XRD) and the accessibility of 
acid sites, hence altered the final acidity profile. For instance, Ni/V2O5 C 
showed the lowest acidity (111 µmol/g) likely due to agglomerated Ni 
species partially blocking acidic sites as a result of using bare stirring. In 
the contrary, Ni/V2O5 M displays the highest acidity (517 µmol/g) 
owing to the rapid nucleation and finer Ni dispersion that preserves 
surface VOH groups while likely exposing additional Lewis sites. Ni/ 
V2O5 S yielded intermediate acidity, consistent with moderate 
improvement in metal distribution. Ni/AC catalysts exhibited a different 
acidity pattern, possessing a substantial amount of strong superacid sites 
(T > 500 ℃). Among these, Ni/AC C showed the highest acidity (1867 
µmol/g), followed by S and M, indicating that conventional stirring, for 
Ni/AC catalyst series, enhanced Ni anchoring and density of accessible 
Brønsted and Lewis superacid sites.

catalysts respectively. For Ni supported on V2O5 catalysts, thermal 
degradation was minimal with a mere total weight loss of 1%. Regarding 
Ni/V2O5, the mass profiles (Fig. S3(e)) of microwave- and convention­

methods produced stable oxide surfaces. As for Ni/AC, the figure shows 
clear synthesis effect on thermal stability of the AC-supported catalyst. 
The mass profiles of these catalysts can be categorized into three distinct 
regions; region I (low temperature) which corresponds to weight loss 
from trapped moisture and volatiles removal, region II (moderate tem­
peratures up to ~ 700 ℃) weight loss in this region is often attributed to 
surface groups decomposition and region III where high temperature 
loss is linked to pyrolysis of more stable carbonaceous species and 
structural changes. Ni/AC M exhibits noticeably greater mass loss than 
Ni/AC C, with the later retaining 10% more weight. This is in agreement 
with reports that microwave treatment introduces additional oxygen­
ated surface groups and disrupts the carbon matrix structure (Aimdate 
et al., 2021).

Based on the product composition obtained from GC–MS result 
(liquid-phase) and GC-TCD (gas-phase), the proposed reaction pathway 
can be outlined as shown in Fig. 2. WCO feedstock was mainly 
comprised of long chain fatty acids including 34% palmitic acid (C16:0) 
and 59% oleic acid (C18:1) (see Table 1). Initially, triglycerides in WCO 
undergo self-hydrolysis via hydrogen abstraction to form glycerol and 
free fatty acids (FFAs), mainly palmitic acid and oleic acid. The FFAs 
adsorbed on Ni/V2O5 surface underwent DO through decarboxylation 
(DCX), decarbonylation (DCO), hydrogenation and cracking pathways. 
High concentration of C2n+1 hydrocarbons detected in the product was 
consistent with the predominance of odd carbon number hydrocarbons 
typically observed during H2-free DO, indicating that DCO, DCX and 
cracking routes dominate over direct HDO (Zulkepli et al., 2018). For 
palmitic acid, DCO proceeds yielding pentadecene (C15H30) as an in­
termediate, after which the C––C bond is subsequently hydrogenated on 
Ni sites to produce pentadecane (C15H32). Alternatively, palmitic acid 
can undergo decarboxylation, directly producing pentadecane without 
olefinic intermediates. Similarly for oleic acid, the DCO pathway yields 
8-Heptadecene (C17H34) which is further hydrogenated to heptadecane 
(C17H36) whereas the DCX route directly produces heptadecane. In 
addition to direct DO, secondary cracking reactions via C––C cleavage 
of fatty acids and long-chain hydrocarbons occur, followed by DO, 
leading to the formation of a mixture of shorter-chain alkanes and al­
kenes. Another minor but detectable pathway involves the hydrogena­
tion of oleic acid to stearic acid (C18:0), followed by C––C bond scission 
and partial hydrogenation to oxygenated intermediates. This route is 
supported by the presence of trace amounts of E-15-Heptadecenal 
(C17H32O). These aldehydes can be further hydrogenated to alcohols 
such as 1-Heptadecanol (C17H36O), which may undergo dehydration 
over acidic sites to generate short-chain olefins (Nugraha et al., 2024). 
At the elevated reaction temperatures, simultaneous C––C bond scis­
sion via β-scission and mild thermal cracking occurs, particularly in the 
presence of acidic V2O5 support sites, which promotes formation of 
shorter chain hydrocarbons (such as 1-octene, octane, 1-nonene, non­
ane, 1-decene, decane, 1-undecene and undecane) and shorter chain 
FFAs, which are then deoxygenated. Note, secondary reactions such as 
water–gas shift (WGS), glycerol reforming and methanation generate in- 
situ H2, enabling hydrogenation of unsaturated fatty acids into saturated 
intermediates and olefins into saturated hydrocarbons (Asikin-Mijan 
et al., 2022). This in-situ H2 phenomenon explains the relatively high 
alkane-to-alkene ratios observed under H2-free DO conditions (Nugraha 
et al., 2024). Furthermore, isomerization, and limited cyclization re­
actions may further transform linear hydrocarbons into branched and 
cyclic species. In line with this, our results show small amounts of cyclic 
compounds (naphthenes), such as n-Nonylcyclohexane (C15H30) reach­
ing up to 6%, consistent with previous studies (Zulkepli et al., 2018). In 
summary, these results highlight the effectiveness of Ni/V2O5 in pro­
moting the efficient DO of –COOH fragments through the DeCOx route 
while minimizing excessive cracking which would otherwise result in 
gaseous light hydrocarbons out of BJF range. The gaseous products were 
evaluated by GC-TCD and depicted in Fig. S6. The gas distribution 
consisted primarily of CO (51.2%), followed by CO2 (21.3%), H2 
(20.3%), and a minor amount of CH4 (7.1%). CO and CO2 detected are 
directly associated with deoxygenation via DCO and DCX pathways, 
respectively. Indeed, the higher content of CO compared to CO2 in­
dicates the oxygen removal occurred mainly via DCO pathway. At the 
same time, CH4 was detected which can be attributed to secondary 
cracking and methanation reactions (Asikin-Mijan et al., 2022). Inter­
estingly, although a fair amount of H2 was detected in the gas phase, the 
reaction is not favoured to have proceeded primarily through the HDO 
pathway, this is supported by the dominance of odd-numbered hydro­
carbons in the liquid product. The detected H2 was likely generated in- 
situ via dehydrogenation by WGS reaction and may have contributed to 
the hydrogenation of intermediate unsaturated hydrocarbons, leading to 
the high selectivity towards saturated alkanes observed in the liquid 
product.
Fig. 1(a) illustrates the use of spectroscopic Fourier Transform 
Infrared Spectroscopy (FTIR), for the qualitative characterization of 
chemical functional groups present in waste cooking oil (WCO) and its 
deoxygenated products. The FTIR spectrum of WCO exhibited distinct 
absorption bands at 3007 cm−1, 2924 cm−1, 2855 cm−1, and 1465 cm−1, 
along with an additional peak at 1377 cm−1, corresponding to the 
stretching and bending vibrations of aliphatic C––H bonds (Hafriz 
et al., 2022). Additionally, absorption peaks at 1746 cm−1 and 1165 
cm−1 were attributed to the carbonyl (C––O) group of glycerides and the 
C––O stretching from ester bonds in WCO, respectively (Khalit et al., 
2021). The deoxygenation process was evidenced by a slight shift of 
the carbonyl (C––O) absorption band from 1745 cm−1 in WCO to 1713 
cm−1 in the deoxygenated product, along with a notable decrease in the 
intensity of the C––O stretching band (at 1163 cm−1 in WCO). This 
spectral transformation indicates the progression of the DO reaction. 
The decrease in peak intensities correlated well with the reduction in 
oxygen content observed in the final product (Aliana-Nasharuddin et al., 
2020). The FTIR analysis of the deoxygenated liquid products revealed 
the presence of alkane and alkene compounds, as indicated by C––H 
stretching vibrations at 2856 cm−1 and 2924 cm−1. Furthermore, the 
emergence of bending vibrations at 1381 cm−1 and 1462 cm−1 for 
alkane –C––H and at 721 cm−1 and 723 cm−1 for alkene –CH=CH– 
confirms the formation of hydrocarbon products following the DO pro­
cess, with more prominent bands for alkane, indicating higher compo­
sition. Furthermore, the absence of a broad, strong peak around 3400 
cm−1 for O––H stretching and another distinct peak of H-O––H 
bending around 1640 cm−1 indicated that no residual water is present 
across all liquid products. This observation confirms that the sample are 
essentially moisture-free as presence of water in the fuel has negative 
effects on engines and fuel systems and therefore hazardous on flight 
safety. Such effects are well documented in the literature which in­
cludes, accelerated corrosion, poorer fuel lubricative properties, mi­
crobial growth, degrades additives and antioxidants and restrict or 
potentially block fuel flow in fuel systems due to icing (Chen et al., 
2022). Overall, the microwave synthesized Ni/V2O5 catalyst was the 
most efficient catalyst in converting the WCO, producing high content of 
hydrocarbons and selective towards BJF fraction, with minimal un­
wanted by-products.

in the DO of WCO was calculated to be 48.3 kJ/mol. This value is lower 
than that reported by Mohmmed et al. of 62.4 kJ/mol for the DO of oleic 
acid over coated Pd/AC (Mohammed et al., 2022) and by Hasanudin 
et al. of 54.3 kJ/mol for the HDO of crude palm oil over Ni/Mo 
ZrO2(Hasanudin et al., 2019). The lower Ed value of this study indicates 
that the catalyst decay of Ni/V2O5 is much less temperature sensitive 
and therefore the catalyst is more stable and robust. Note, Fig. 3b-c 
shows the weight loss curve in the TGA/O2 analysis and RAMAN spectra 
of spent catalyst after kinetic study, respectively. The weight loss 
observed in the 200–400 ℃ region is attributed to the combustion of soft 
amorphous coke which is known to strongly poison catalyst active sites 
and thus cause rapid loss of catalyst activity (Li et al., 2017). In contrast, 
weight loss occurring at temperatures above 400 ℃ is assumed to 
resemble hard coke of graphitic structure, although these deposits are 
more difficult to remove during regeneration, they are generally much 
less immediately deactivating (Shakor et al., 2024). Hard coke pre­
dominated the carbon deposits on Ni/V2O5 as evident from the weight 
drop after 400℃. The RAMAN spectrum provided further complemen­
tary evidence, which displayed a pronounced G band (~1596 cm−1) and 
an extremely weak D band (~1350 cm−1). Obviously, the low D/G in­
tensity ratio points to the presence of more ordered graphitic carbon. 
These findings indicate that catalyst deactivation was mainly caused by 
carbon deposition of catalysts surface, which likely blocked active sites 
and reduced catalytic activity.
4. Conclusions
In this study, the catalytic deoxygenation of WCO to jet-like hydro­
carbons under H2-free conditions was investigated and compared over a 
series of transition metal-based catalysts, including a novel Ni/V2O5 
catalyst, synthesised via wet-impregnation and subjected to different 
treatments. The synthesized catalysts by microwave- and sonication- 
assistance demonstrated improved physico-chemical properties which 
contributed to their high reaction performance. These enhanced 
methods reduced synthesis time and energy consumption while deliv­
ering comparable or superior catalytic performance. Ni/V2O5 M 
exhibited the best performance with a hydrocarbon yield of 96% and a 
jet-selectivity of 78% consisting mainly of desirable n-paraffins. The 
synergistic effect between Ni and V2O5 lead to formation of rich oxygen 
vacancies that enhanced the DeCOx activity and produced favourable
