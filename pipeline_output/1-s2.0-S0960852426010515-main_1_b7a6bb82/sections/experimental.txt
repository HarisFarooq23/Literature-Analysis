intermediates mainly fatty aldehydes and carboxylic acids. However, Ni

activity, highlighting the essential role of Ni in promoting C-–O bond 
cleavage. Nonetheless, Ni/AC catalyst series lack oxygen lattice, even 
though trace amounts of NiO are present, there is effectively limited Ov 
that exist. Therefore, Ni/AC catalyst relies mainly on its metallic Ni sites 
and high acidity to cleave C-–O but also risks faster deactivation, in the 
absence of H2, by coke deposition leading to severe blockage of active Ni 
sites.

also be utilized to determine the effectiveness of catalyzed reactions in 
terms of DO activity or the extent of oxygen-containing compounds 
removal, as shown in the product distribution results (Fig. 1(e)). Blank

blank yielded lowest n-paraffin. It is well known that during non-

Based on the product composition obtained from GC–MS result 
(liquid-phase) and GC-TCD (gas-phase), the proposed reaction pathway 
can be outlined as shown in Fig. 2. WCO feedstock was mainly 
comprised of long chain fatty acids including 34% palmitic acid (C16:0) 
and 59% oleic acid (C18:1) (see Table 1). Initially, triglycerides in WCO 
undergo self-hydrolysis via hydrogen abstraction to form glycerol and 
free fatty acids (FFAs), mainly palmitic acid and oleic acid. The FFAs 
adsorbed on Ni/V2O5 surface underwent DO through decarboxylation 
(DCX), decarbonylation (DCO), hydrogenation and cracking pathways. 
High concentration of C2n+1 hydrocarbons detected in the product was 
consistent with the predominance of odd carbon number hydrocarbons 
typically observed during H2-free DO, indicating that DCO, DCX and 
cracking routes dominate over direct HDO (Zulkepli et al., 2018). For 
palmitic acid, DCO proceeds yielding pentadecene (C15H30) as an in­
termediate, after which the C––C bond is subsequently hydrogenated on 
Ni sites to produce pentadecane (C15H32). Alternatively, palmitic acid 
can undergo decarboxylation, directly producing pentadecane without 
olefinic intermediates. Similarly for oleic acid, the DCO pathway yields 
8-Heptadecene (C17H34) which is further hydrogenated to heptadecane 
(C17H36) whereas the DCX route directly produces heptadecane. In 
addition to direct DO, secondary cracking reactions via C––C cleavage 
of fatty acids and long-chain hydrocarbons occur, followed by DO, 
leading to the formation of a mixture of shorter-chain alkanes and al­
kenes. Another minor but detectable pathway involves the hydrogena­
tion of oleic acid to stearic acid (C18:0), followed by C––C bond scission 
and partial hydrogenation to oxygenated intermediates. This route is 
supported by the presence of trace amounts of E-15-Heptadecenal 
(C17H32O). These aldehydes can be further hydrogenated to alcohols 
such as 1-Heptadecanol (C17H36O), which may undergo dehydration 
over acidic sites to generate short-chain olefins (Nugraha et al., 2024). 
At the elevated reaction temperatures, simultaneous C––C bond scis­
sion via β-scission and mild thermal cracking occurs, particularly in the 
presence of acidic V2O5 support sites, which promotes formation of 
shorter chain hydrocarbons (such as 1-octene, octane, 1-nonene, non­
ane, 1-decene, decane, 1-undecene and undecane) and shorter chain 
FFAs, which are then deoxygenated. Note, secondary reactions such as 
water–gas shift (WGS), glycerol reforming and methanation generate in- 
situ H2, enabling hydrogenation of unsaturated fatty acids into saturated 
intermediates and olefins into saturated hydrocarbons (Asikin-Mijan 
et al., 2022). This in-situ H2 phenomenon explains the relatively high 
alkane-to-alkene ratios observed under H2-free DO conditions (Nugraha 
et al., 2024). Furthermore, isomerization, and limited cyclization re­
actions may further transform linear hydrocarbons into branched and 
cyclic species. In line with this, our results show small amounts of cyclic 
compounds (naphthenes), such as n-Nonylcyclohexane (C15H30) reach­
ing up to 6%, consistent with previous studies (Zulkepli et al., 2018). In 
summary, these results highlight the effectiveness of Ni/V2O5 in pro­
moting the efficient DO of –COOH fragments through the DeCOx route 
while minimizing excessive cracking which would otherwise result in 
gaseous light hydrocarbons out of BJF range. The gaseous products were 
evaluated by GC-TCD and depicted in Fig. S6. The gas distribution 
consisted primarily of CO (51.2%), followed by CO2 (21.3%), H2 
(20.3%), and a minor amount of CH4 (7.1%). CO and CO2 detected are 
directly associated with deoxygenation via DCO and DCX pathways, 
respectively. Indeed, the higher content of CO compared to CO2 in­
dicates the oxygen removal occurred mainly via DCO pathway. At the 
same time, CH4 was detected which can be attributed to secondary 
cracking and methanation reactions (Asikin-Mijan et al., 2022). Inter­
estingly, although a fair amount of H2 was detected in the gas phase, the 
reaction is not favoured to have proceeded primarily through the HDO 
pathway, this is supported by the dominance of odd-numbered hydro­
carbons in the liquid product. The detected H2 was likely generated in- 
situ via dehydrogenation by WGS reaction and may have contributed to 
the hydrogenation of intermediate unsaturated hydrocarbons, leading to 
the high selectivity towards saturated alkanes observed in the liquid 
product.
Fig. 1(a) illustrates the use of spectroscopic Fourier Transform 
Infrared Spectroscopy (FTIR), for the qualitative characterization of 
chemical functional groups present in waste cooking oil (WCO) and its 
deoxygenated products. The FTIR spectrum of WCO exhibited distinct 
absorption bands at 3007 cm−1, 2924 cm−1, 2855 cm−1, and 1465 cm−1, 
along with an additional peak at 1377 cm−1, corresponding to the 
stretching and bending vibrations of aliphatic C––H bonds (Hafriz 
et al., 2022). Additionally, absorption peaks at 1746 cm−1 and 1165 
cm−1 were attributed to the carbonyl (C––O) group of glycerides and the 
C––O stretching from ester bonds in WCO, respectively (Khalit et al., 
2021). The deoxygenation process was evidenced by a slight shift of 
the carbonyl (C––O) absorption band from 1745 cm−1 in WCO to 1713 
cm−1 in the deoxygenated product, along with a notable decrease in the 
intensity of the C––O stretching band (at 1163 cm−1 in WCO). This 
spectral transformation indicates the progression of the DO reaction. 
The decrease in peak intensities correlated well with the reduction in 
oxygen content observed in the final product (Aliana-Nasharuddin et al., 
2020). The FTIR analysis of the deoxygenated liquid products revealed 
the presence of alkane and alkene compounds, as indicated by C––H 
stretching vibrations at 2856 cm−1 and 2924 cm−1. Furthermore, the 
emergence of bending vibrations at 1381 cm−1 and 1462 cm−1 for 
alkane –C––H and at 721 cm−1 and 723 cm−1 for alkene –CH=CH– 
confirms the formation of hydrocarbon products following the DO pro­
cess, with more prominent bands for alkane, indicating higher compo­
sition. Furthermore, the absence of a broad, strong peak around 3400 
cm−1 for O––H stretching and another distinct peak of H-O––H 
bending around 1640 cm−1 indicated that no residual water is present 
across all liquid products. This observation confirms that the sample are 
essentially moisture-free as presence of water in the fuel has negative 
effects on engines and fuel systems and therefore hazardous on flight 
safety. Such effects are well documented in the literature which in­
cludes, accelerated corrosion, poorer fuel lubricative properties, mi­
crobial growth, degrades additives and antioxidants and restrict or 
potentially block fuel flow in fuel systems due to icing (Chen et al., 
2022). Overall, the microwave synthesized Ni/V2O5 catalyst was the 
most efficient catalyst in converting the WCO, producing high content of 
hydrocarbons and selective towards BJF fraction, with minimal un­
wanted by-products.
3.3. Performance comparison of non-noble metal catalyst for WCO 
deoxygenation with present study
The deoxygenation performance obtained in this study is compared 
with similar Ni-based catalysts/WCO feedstocks systems from previous 
literature (Table 5). In this study, using a microwave-assisted Ni/V2O5 
catalyst, a 96% hydrocarbon yield with 78% BJF selectivity was ob­
tained from the DO of WCO at 400 ℃ over 5 wt% for 2 h. Hafriz et al., 
2022 utilized a low-cost NiO dolomite-based catalyst on WCO, however

the catalyst exhibited lower hydrocarbon yield and selectivity due to its 
limited acidity and thermal stability as structure collapses of dolomite 
catalyst occurs at high temperatures (>400 ℃). Several studies inves­
tigated the HDO of WCO over Ni-Mo/alumina-silica catalyst. For

industrial scale-up. Therefore, the Ni/V2O5 catalyst developed in this 
study demonstrates strong potential for commercial production of BJF 
due to its use of inexpensive materials, simple preparation method, good 
catalyst stability and superior catalytic activity.
3.4. Kinetic deactivation study on DO of WCO over Ni/V2O5 microwave- 
synthesised catalyst
The catalyst stability of Ni/V2O5 M was evaluated at 380––420 ℃. 
The Arrhenius plot of lnkd against 1/T was constructed based on Eq.6 
producing a linear relationship from which the activation energy of 
deactivation (Ed) was determined (see Fig. 3 (d)). The strong linearity 
obtained (R2 = 0.94) indicates that the deactivation rate constant fol­
lows Arrhenius behaviour and that the reaction kinetics are well 
described by Arrhenius equation. From the graph, the Ed of Ni/V2O5 M 
in the DO of WCO was calculated to be 48.3 kJ/mol. This value is lower 
than that reported by Mohmmed et al. of 62.4 kJ/mol for the DO of oleic 
acid over coated Pd/AC (Mohammed et al., 2022) and by Hasanudin 
et al. of 54.3 kJ/mol for the HDO of crude palm oil over Ni/Mo 
ZrO2(Hasanudin et al., 2019). The lower Ed value of this study indicates 
that the catalyst decay of Ni/V2O5 is much less temperature sensitive 
and therefore the catalyst is more stable and robust. Note, Fig. 3b-c 
shows the weight loss curve in the TGA/O2 analysis and RAMAN spectra 
of spent catalyst after kinetic study, respectively. The weight loss 
observed in the 200–400 ℃ region is attributed to the combustion of soft 
amorphous coke which is known to strongly poison catalyst active sites 
and thus cause rapid loss of catalyst activity (Li et al., 2017). In contrast, 
weight loss occurring at temperatures above 400 ℃ is assumed to 
resemble hard coke of graphitic structure, although these deposits are 
more difficult to remove during regeneration, they are generally much 
less immediately deactivating (Shakor et al., 2024). Hard coke pre­
dominated the carbon deposits on Ni/V2O5 as evident from the weight 
drop after 400℃. The RAMAN spectrum provided further complemen­
tary evidence, which displayed a pronounced G band (~1596 cm−1) and 
an extremely weak D band (~1350 cm−1). Obviously, the low D/G in­
tensity ratio points to the presence of more ordered graphitic carbon. 
These findings indicate that catalyst deactivation was mainly caused by 
carbon deposition of catalysts surface, which likely blocked active sites 
and reduced catalytic activity.
