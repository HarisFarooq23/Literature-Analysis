The transport sector plays a significant role in the global dynamics of modern societies,
directly enabling the movement of people, goods, and services. Aviation, in particular,
has undergone substantial market expansion over the past two decades, establishing
itself as one of the most relevant economic activities worldwide. This growth has been
accompanied by notable socioeconomic impacts, primarily driven by the rise in middle-
class purchasing power and the reduction in airfares. In 2023, airlines around the world
transported approximately 4.4 billion passengers and more than 61 million tons of cargo.
These numbers are expected to grow significantly, with forecasts indicating that global air
travel could reach close to 10 billion passengers by 2050 [1].
Despite the growth of aviation and the various benefits it brings to society, environ-
mental concerns related to this activity require careful attention. While aviation facilitates

increasingly critical [4].
The International Air Transport Association (IATA) has set a target to reduce CO2
emissions by 50% in 2050, using 2005 levels as a baseline. Although advancements in air-
craft design over recent decades—driven by developments in aviation engineering—have
improved fuel efficiency, they are still not sufficient to fully decarbonize the sector [5,6]. In
this context, sustainable aviation fuel (SAF), also known as bio-jet fuel, is one of the most
promising alternatives to achieve the required CO2 reduction target defined by IATA [5,7].
Biofuels represent a closed carbon cycle, as part of the CO2 released into the atmosphere
is neutralized by the CO2 absorbed during the growth of the plants used to produce the
fuel [8].
SAF can mainly be produced from biomass and waste, including feedstock rich in
lipids, such as vegetable oils, animal fats, and waste cooking oil (WCO). These sources are
particularly attractive for sustainable fuel production due to their high concentrations of
triglycerides and free fatty acids, typically composed of carbon chains ranging from C16 to
C22 [9]. Through catalytic processing, these long-chain molecules can be converted into
hydrocarbons within the C8 to C16 range, which corresponds to the carbon distribution
required for jet fuel. The use of lipid-based biomass thus represents a key strategy for
achieving climate change mitigation targets by enabling the production of drop-in fuels
compatible with existing aviation infrastructure [5,10,11].
In addition to lipid-based feedstocks, the limited availability of WCO and other
biogenic resources poses a challenge for meeting the long-term demand for SAF at an
industrial scale. In this context, the valorization of other feedstocks, particularly plastic
residues, emerges as a complementary and strategic route for aviation fuel production.
Plastic wastes are composed predominantly of carbon and hydrogen, exhibit high energy
density, and are generated in large quantities worldwide, making them attractive alternative
feedstocks for hydrocarbon fuel production [12,13]. Their catalytic conversion into jet-
fuel-range hydrocarbons mainly addresses critical environmental issues associated with
plastic waste accumulation and disposal [14]. Therefore, extending the scope of this
work to include the catalytic upgrading of plastic residues aligns with the dual objective
of producing C8-C16 hydrocarbons while promoting circular economy principles and
environmentally responsible waste management.
From a process perspective, both lipid-based feedstock and plastic residues require
catalytic upgrading to produce fuels that meet aviation specifications. Developing an
efficient, low-cost, and effective catalytic system is a key focus for producing aviation
fuel from renewable sources. Currently, lipid-derived sources cannot be used directly
as drop-in fuels due to their high oxygen content, which lowers the high heating value
(HHV) [15]. A higher HHV is desirable, as it improves fuel combustion efficiency. In
addition, the high oxygen content leads to other drawbacks such as low chemical stability,
high viscosity, strong acidity, and corrosiveness, which present challenges for storage and
transportation [15–17]. To overcome these limitations, additional upgrading reactions, such
as cracking and isomerization, are required to improve the physicochemical properties of

the fuel and adjust the carbon chain length to meet jet fuel specifications [18]. Similarly,
plastic waste must undergo controlled catalytic conversion to adjust the hydrocarbon chain
length, promote isomerization, and adjust fuel properties [19,20]. Although lipid-based
feedstocks and plastic residues differ in structure, they share important similarities in
their catalytic upgrading pathways. For triglycerides and fatty acids, conversion into
jet-fuel-range hydrocarbons involves oxygen removal via hydrodeoxygenation, followed
by C–C bond cleavage to adjust the carbon chain length. In contrast, plastics such as
polyolefins are already oxygen-free; however, their conversion into C8–C16 hydrocarbons
relies primarily on hydrogenolysis and hydrocracking, which also involve C–C bond
cleavage. Despite these compositional differences, both systems depend on similar catalytic
functionalities, particularly the balance between metal and acid sites, to achieve the desired
product distribution.
Several catalysts have been investigated to improve selectivity toward hydrocarbons
found in SAF, including transition metals (Ni, Co, Mo, and Fe) and noble metals (Pt,
Pd, and Ru) supported on materials such as zeolites, oxides, mesoporous silicas, and
carbon materials [9,21–25]. However, their low selectivity toward SAF-range fractions,
high operational costs, and fast deactivation make their use less advantageous. Moreover,
the high acidity of some of these materials can promote the overcracking of the substrate
into small fractions, such as gasoline. On the other hand, the development of efficient
bifunctional catalysts capable of balancing metal and acid functionalities has emerged as a
key strategy to enhance catalytic performance and improve selectivity toward jet-fuel-range
hydrocarbons. Given these limitations, carbon-based materials such as carbon nanotubes
(CNT) have been proposed as alternative catalytic supports [26–30]. CNT offers a high
surface area and excellent thermal and chemical stability, allowing good dispersion of
active metal phases [31]. In addition, the ease of introducing oxygenated groups on its
surface, enhancing CNT’s acidity, makes these materials an interesting choice to use for
cracking and isomerization reactions [32]. These features contribute to higher selectivity
toward jet fuel-range hydrocarbons, improved resistance to deactivation, and the potential
to reduce operational costs.
In this context, this mini-review summarizes recent advances in catalytic pathways for
SAF production from lipid-based feedstocks and plastic waste, with particular emphasis on
hydroprocessing reactions and the design of bifunctional heterogeneous catalysts, focusing
on advances in the use and catalytic performance of carbon-based materials in the conver-
sion of lipid feedstocks and polyolefins. This study provides a comparative analysis of both
routes, highlighting common catalytic challenges and opportunities. It further emphasizes
the role of carbon-based materials as catalyst supports and discusses how their properties
influence catalytic performance across different reaction systems.
2. Sustainable Aviation Fuel: An Alternative Solution to the Energy
Transition in Aviation
The aviation sector faces major challenges in reducing its CO2 emissions. Air transport
depends almost entirely on liquid hydrocarbon fuels because of their high energy content
and storage stability. Unlike ground transport, the electrification of aviation is still far from
being feasible for commercial aircraft [33]. As a result, aviation remains strongly dependent
on fossil jet fuels, and its carbon footprint continues to grow with the increasing global
demand for air travel [10,33,34].
In response to this challenge, international aviation governance bodies have estab-
lished increasingly stringent environmental targets. The International Civil Aviation Or-
ganization (ICAO) has introduced the Carbon Offsetting and Reduction Scheme for Inter-
national Aviation (CORSIA), a global program aiming to achieve carbon-neutral growth

Current projections by IATA indicate different contributions to decrease carbon emis-

SAF has a hydrocarbon composition similar to fossil-based jet fuel, which leads to
comparable exhaust emission profiles [38]. Currently, it is approved for use in blends of
up to 50% with conventional jet fuel. However, there is still no technology commercially
certified for producing 100% synthetic drop-in fuels (i.e., fuels that are fully compatible
with existing aircraft engines and fuel distribution systems) [39].
SAF is composed of the same hydrocarbon range found in conventional jet fuel,
typically from C8 to C16 [40]. Its composition is predominantly n-paraffins and iso-paraffins
(65–75%), followed by aromatic compounds (10–25%), cycloalkanes (10–20%), and a small
fraction of alkenes (<1%) [40]. It can be produced by a diverse number of renewable sources,
such as oils and grease, waste cooking oil, algae oil, alcohols, urban waste, lignocellulosic
biomass, and even CO2 capture [41–43]. SAF can be considered sustainable as long as
the feedstock does not compete with food crops or agricultural outputs, or it does not
require additional resources such as water and land expansion. More broadly, SAF avoids
environmental impacts such as deforestation, loss of soil productivity, and decline in
biodiversity. Different from fossil fuels, which introduce new CO2 into the atmosphere
by releasing carbon, SAF operate within a closed carbon cycle: the CO2 emitted during
combustion is approximately equal to the amount previously absorbed by the biomass
utilized as feedstock during its growth [33,36].

To produce SAF that meets the specifications required for commercial aviation fuels,
several technological pathways can be employed using a wide range of renewable feed-
stocks. These feedstocks are commonly classified into different generations according to
their origin, sustainability profile, and impact on land use and food supply. Each genera-
tion is associated with specific conversion technologies and yields distinct fuel properties,
making the selection of feedstock a critical factor in both the technical and environmental
performance of jet fuel from non-petrochemical sources. Figure 2 illustrates the potential
sources that can be used to obtain this biofuel. Each generation is also associated with a spe-
cific type of biofuel. For example, first-generation biomass is converted into first-generation
biofuels, and this pattern continues across subsequent generations. First-generation feed-
stocks consist of edible crops rich in oils, sugars, and starches, such as vegetable oils, wheat,
maize, and other agricultural products. Although the easy accessibility, these kinds of
sources compete largely with the food supply. Second-generation feedstocks are obtained
from non-edible lignocellulosic biomass, including agricultural residues, non-food crops,
and municipal or industrial wastes. Because they originate from residues, these feedstocks
offer the advantage of not competing with the food supply. However, their use is con-
strained by significant drawbacks, including fluctuations in feedstock quality and limited
availability. Third-generation feedstocks are derived from macro- and microalgae, which
represent emerging sources that do not compete with arable land or freshwater resources.
Nevertheless, they require a high energy supply. Finally, fourth-generation feedstocks
involve advanced pathways that include the use of unconventional biomass and the capture
of atmospheric CO2 through carbon capture technologies [15,41,46].
Each feedstock type requires a specific technological pathway to convert it into the
desired SAF hydrocarbon range. Currently, five routes enclosing nine production technolo-

routes are summarized in Table 2. These pathways differ in terms of feedstock compatibil-
ity, conversion chemistry, fuel yield, and sustainability performance, and they define the
allowable blending limits and final fuel specifications.

2.1. Oil-to-Jet
The oil-to-jet pathway encloses technologies that convert lipid-based feedstocks, such
as vegetable oils, animal fats, waste cooking oil, and algae, into sustainable aviation fuel
through hydroprocessing and related upgrading steps. In the hydroprocessed esters and
fatty acids (HEFA)—synthetic paraffinic kerosene (SPK), lipid sources are hydrotreated
into SAF. The resulting fuel can be blended with conventional jet fuel in concentrations
of up to 50%. This process provides superior energy conversion efficiency among the

other pathways but is limited to oil feedstocks only [34]. A similar approach is used in
the SPK from hydrocarbons obtained by HEFA (HC-HEFA-SPK), which applies the same
hydrotreatment principle, but the feedstock target is mainly algae. As discussed above,
algae have a high growth rate and do not compete with food. On the other hand, it can
only be blended with petroleum-based aviation fuel up to 10% [40]. SPK from catalytic
hydrothermolysis (CHJ-SPK) uses a hydrothermal process to convert oils and fats into
hydrocarbons under high temperature and pressure, followed by upgrading through
hydrotreatment. This pathway produces hydrocarbons with a molecular composition and
chemical properties nearly identical to those found in petroleum-derived jet fuel, which
ensures full compatibility with existing aviation fuel infrastructure. However, the process
is limited by its high energy consumption due to the severe operating conditions required
for hydrothermal conversion [47].
2.2. Gas-to-Jet
The gas-to-jet route is based on the conversion of biomass, wastes, captured CO2, or
other carbon-rich material into syngas (CO+H2), which is subsequently transformed into
SAF hydrocarbons via Fischer–Tropsch (FT) synthesis and downstream upgrading. The
production of SPK by hydroprocessing of Fischer–Tropsch products (FT-SPK) converts
the referred feedstocks, which are then catalytically upgraded into synthetic kerosene,
approved for blending up to 50% [48]. Technologies based on atmospheric CO2 capture
coupled with reverse water–gas shift reactions are gaining increasing attention as a potential
route for Fischer–Tropsch-based SAF production. However, they are not yet approved by
ASTM (e-fuel production) [47]. In this case, CO2 reacts with H2 (produced from water
electrolysis), producing CO and water. The CO produced is then hydrogenated, producing
liquid fuels by Fischer–Tropsch synthesis [49]. Another variation in this route, known as
SPK by Fischer–Tropsch with alkylation of light aromatics from non-petroleum sources
(FT-SPK+A), incorporates an additional step to produce synthetic aromatics, improving
compatibility with current aviation fuel systems [46]. Despite their ability to produce
high-quality SPK, FT-SPK, and FT-SPK+A, they are limited by high capital costs, complex
process configurations, energy-intensive conversion steps, and the need for blending with
conventional jet fuel due to low aromatic content [47].
2.3. Alcohol-to-Jet
The alcohol-to-jet pathway (ATJ-SPK) involves the transformation of bio-derived
alcohols, such as ethanol and isobutanol, produced from sugars or lignocellulosic biomass
into SAF-range hydrocarbons through dehydration, oligomerization, and hydrotreatment
processes. This route also includes the production of synthetic aromatic kerosene from ATJ
(ATJ-SKA) to meet the minimum aromatic content required in aviation fuels [50]. Although
the ATJ-SPK and ATJ-SKA pathways provide synthetic routes to produce paraffinic and
aromatic hydrocarbons from bio-derived alcohols, their commercialization is limited by
high production costs, restricted feedstock availability (mainly ethanol and isobutanol),
energy-intensive conversion steps, and low overall fuel yield [51].
2.4. Sugar-to-Jet
The direct sugars-to-hydrocarbons pathway by synthesized iso-paraffins (SIP-SPK)
employs engineered microorganisms to convert sugars into renewable iso-paraffinic hy-
drocarbons, which are subsequently upgraded to meet aviation specifications. These
iso-paraffinic compounds exhibit higher energy density compared to ATJ-derived fuels,
lower pollutant emissions, and combustion behavior similar to that of conventional jet fuel.
Nevertheless, this pathway faces several limitations, including a product with high viscos-
ity and poor combustion performance in turbine engines, depending on costly feedstocks,

2.5. Co-Processing
Finally, co-processing technologies allow renewable feedstocks such as lipid sources
and treated biomass to be processed together with petroleum streams in existing refinery
units, resulting in partially renewable jet fuel with blending limits ranging from 5 to 10%.
This pathway enables the production of lower-carbon-intensity fuels, leverages existing
petroleum refinery infrastructure, and offers flexibility to process a wide range of biogenic
feedstocks. Yet, co-processing requires efficient oxygen removal and is associated with
operational challenges related to feedstock variability, blend ratios, and product property
fluctuations, which can impact process stability and fuel quality [47,54].
Among all ASTM-approved pathways, the HEFA route currently stands out as the
most commercially mature and widely deployed option for SAF production [43]. Unlike
other pathways that are still limited by low technology readiness levels, high capital costs, or
strict feedstock constraints, the HEFA process benefits from existing refinery infrastructure,
scalable feedstock supply chains, and proven compatibility with aviation fuel specifica-
tions [55]. As a result, HEFA-derived fuels account for the majority of global SAF production
and are projected to remain the dominant pathway in the short-to-medium term.
Given its industrial relevance and strategic role in aviation decarbonization, the
hydroprocessing of lipid-based feedstocks to produce SAF-range hydrocarbons has gained
significant attention from both the scientific community and the aviation fuel industry.
A wide range of renewable lipids can be used as feedstocks, particularly waste cooking
oil classified as a second-generation resource. However, several challenges remain to
ensure their suitability for aviation use, including the need to meet strict physicochemical
requirements, reduce production costs, and improve overall process efficiency.
Moreover, the HEFA pathway has emerged as the most strategically relevant route for
short-term implementation of SAF, as it is currently the only production technology operat-
ing on a commercial scale. Its implementation has been supported by regulatory policies
and market incentives favoring drop-in fuels compatible with existing aircraft engines and
fuel distribution infrastructure. As airlines and fuel suppliers move toward established
decarbonization targets, understanding the HEFA process becomes essential to assessing its
technical maturity, economic viability, and potential for large-scale commercialization. The
next section provides a detailed analysis of this pathway, including conversion mechanisms
and upgrading strategies required to meet aviation fuel specifications.
3. Hydroprocessing of Lipid-Based Sources
As discussed in the previous section, lipid-based feedstocks can be hydroprocessed
through different technological oil-to-jet routes for the production of SAF. A fundamental
aspect in understanding the conversion of these materials into liquid hydrocarbons is their
chemical composition. Most lipid feedstocks, such as vegetable oils, animal fats, and waste
cooking oil (WCO), are predominantly composed of triacylglycerols, classified as simple
or neutral lipids, in which a glycerol backbone is esterified with three fatty acid chains
(Figure 3). Free fatty acids are also present, especially in thermally degraded or previously
processed sources [56].
The carbon chain presented in these lipid structures typically ranges from 14 to
22 carbon atoms [9]. Filho et al. [57] investigated the fatty acid composition of soybean oil,
palm oil, and WCO. The results demonstrated that soybean oil and WCO are predominantly
composed of C18 fatty acids, mainly linoleic and oleic acids, whereas palm oil is rich in C16
fatty acids, primarily palmitic acid. Minor fractions of other fatty acids ranging from C12 to

C24 were also identified. This carbon chain distribution closely matches the hydrocarbon
range required for jet fuel production, reinforcing the suitability of lipid-based feedstocks
as promising precursors for the generation of liquid transportation fuels.

The use of oils and fats for biodiesel production via transesterification reactions has
been extensively studied over the past decade [58,59]. However, the transesterification
route leads to the formation of oxygenated esters (fatty acid alkyl esters), decreasing some
physicochemical properties required for fuels. In this scenario, hydrotreated vegetable oil
processes have gained significant attention for the production of green diesel, as the removal
of oxygen from triglycerides and free fatty acids results in hydrocarbons with superior
fuel properties [60]. This hydroprocessing approach can also be targeted at the aviation
fuel range, in which case the process is referred to as HEFA, enabling the production of
sustainable SAF compliant with ASTM specifications.
Hydrotreating lipid-based sources plays an important role in the final quality parame-
ters of SAF. The removal of heteroatoms—mainly oxygen—enhances the chemical stability,
decreases the viscosity, the acidity, and the corrosiveness [15,18]. Another critical property
is the HHV, which is directly associated with the degree of deoxygenation. Since oxy-
genated compounds exhibit lower energy density, their conversion into fully deoxygenated
hydrocarbons significantly increases the HHV, resulting in a fuel with greater combustion
efficiency and improved overall energy performance [61].
Since oils and fats cannot be directly used as fuels, these feedstocks must undergo
the HEFA processing stages to meet the fuel quality and commercialization parameters
previously described (Table 1). This SAF production route comprises several steps, as
illustrated in Figure 4. Initially, lipid feedstock is pretreated to remove any impurities
(e.g., solids, phospholipids, sulfur, water). After that, the pretreated oil is subjected to
hydrotreatment for the removal of heteroatoms, mainly oxygen. Deoxygenation reactions
occur in the presence of hydrogen and catalysts, producing deoxygenated compounds that
are subsequently directed to the cracking and isomerization stages, where hydrogen and
catalysts are also employed [18].
The cracking step is essential to adjust the liquid hydrocarbon fraction within the
carbon number range required for jet fuel, since the sole deoxygenation of triglycerides
typically yields products within the diesel range (C15–C22). Isomerization reactions, in turn,
are required to introduce branching into the hydrocarbon chains, thereby improving the
freezing point and cold-flow properties of the renewable aviation fuel [11].

A typical HEFA process results in a mixture of liquid hydrocarbons ranging from the
gasoline to the diesel fractions, as well as gas products (C1–C4 compounds). Ideally, the
process should maximize the production of hydrocarbons within the C8–C16 range. There-
fore, careful control of the cracking reactions is essential to prevent excessive chain scission,
which would otherwise increase the formation of lighter hydrocarbons (gasoline and gases)
and reduce overall liquid yield. Thus, the balance between deoxygenation, cracking, and
isomerization reactions must be optimized to achieve the desired fuel properties while
maintaining high conversion efficiency.
3.1. Deoxygenation
Deoxygenation reactions are a well-established reaction in petrochemical refineries,
which are used to remove the oxygen content in petroleum derivatives [62]. Overall, lipid
sources have 40% of oxygen content, which makes the removal a great challenge to turn oil
into a drop-in fuel [18].
In this context, different reaction pathways have been reported in the literature for the
deoxygenation of lipid-based compounds: hydrodeoxygenation (HDO), decarbonylation
(DCO), and decarboxylation (DCO2) [63]. The reaction mechanisms of these routes are
illustrated in Figure 5 [64]. Considering stearic acid (a fatty acid molecule used as a
model compound for lipidic biomass), the three reaction routes lead to oxygen removal
through distinct chemical transformations. Stearic acid can first be deoxygenated via
the DCO2 route, producing n-heptadecane and CO2, or through the HDO pathway. In
the HDO route, the fatty acid is hydrogenated, releasing the first oxygen atom in the
form of water. Subsequently, the corresponding aldehyde (octadecanal) can either lose
its oxygen atom via the DCO route—resulting in n-heptadecane and CO—or continue
undergoing HDO reactions. Another oxygen atom is then removed as water, forming
the corresponding alcohol (octadecanol), which is further hydrogenated to produce the
n-alkane (n-octadecane). Overall, each molecule of fatty acid requires three moles of
hydrogen, producing the corresponding alkane and two moles of water through the HDO
route [65]. In contrast, the DCO and DCO2 pathways release GHG and result in a reduction
in one carbon atom in the hydrocarbon chain [11].

Side reactions may occur in which the CO and CO2 produced via the DCO/DCO2
routes participate in reversible reactions with H2, leading to the formation of additional
CH4 and water through methanation and water–gas shift reactions [66].
These reactions usually occur simultaneously during hydrotreatment, and their pre-
dominance depends on the catalyst type, reaction temperature, hydrogen pressure, and
feedstock composition [67,68]. Nevertheless, the HDO route stands out from DCO and
DCO2 because it does not generate CO or CO2, representing a more environmentally benign
pathway by minimizing GHG emissions [62].
Catalysts play a crucial role in deoxygenation reactions. In general, supported mono-
or bimetallic catalysts are widely employed, with transition metals such as Ni, Co, Mo, Fe,
and W being the most common [69–71]. Noble metals like Pt and Pd also exhibit excellent
catalytic performance but are often avoided due to their high cost [25,72]. The metal must
be well dispersed on the support and highly reduced to enhance the hydrogen dissociation
on the metal phase. In bimetallic catalysts, electron transfer between the two metals can
improve the reducibility of the active phase and promote hydrogen activation. Therefore,
promoters such as Ni and Co are often incorporated together with Mo (in its oxide or
sulfide form) or W to enhance the catalytic activity [61].
In addition to the metal phase, the selection of appropriate support is essential. One
of the most important properties of a support for lipid deoxygenation reactions is its
mesoporous nature, which facilitates the diffusion of large molecules such as triglycerides
and fatty acids, minimizing external mass-transfer limitations. Accordingly, metal oxides
(Al2O3, SiO2, TiO2, and ZrO2) and mesoporous silicas (SBA-15, MCM-41, and MCM-48) are
commonly used as supports in the deoxygenation of lipid feedstocks [9,11,24].
Deoxygenation reactions typically require temperatures in the range of 300–420 ◦C for
the hydrodeoxygenation of oils and somewhat lower temperatures (250–360 ◦C) when fatty
acids or their derivatives are used as substrates [63]. Typical H2 pressures range from 20 to
50 bar for oil feedstocks and from 10 to 40 bar for fatty acids [61]. Although this process is
widely applied in industry, the need for high hydrogen pressures to ensure efficient C–O
bond cleavage is not advantageous in terms of cost and safety. Furthermore, coke formation
during the reaction can lead to catalyst deactivation [73].

crease the formation of lighter alkanes, improving the selectivity in the desired n-C8-C16
hydrocarbons. On the other hand, the isomerization reaction can process those normal alka-
nes into branched hydrocarbons (iso-alkanes). Increasing the ratio iso-alkanes/n-alkanes
(iso/n) is extremely relevant in terms of fuel performance, mainly to the freezing and flash
point [61,73,74]. One advantage is that both cracking and isomerization can occur simulta-
neously over bifunctional supported catalysts. Once deoxygenation reactions are catalyzed
on the catalyst’s metal sites, forming n-alkanes, cracking and isomerization reactions take
place more commonly in the support’s acid sites [74].
The classical reaction mechanism for cracking and isomerization takes place over bifunc-
tional catalysts, which contain metallic sites that promote hydrogenation/dehydrogenation
reactions and acid sites responsible for cracking and isomerization [75]. Initially, the
n-alkane is dehydrogenated on the metallic sites, forming the corresponding n-alkene
(Figure 6). The olefin then migrates to the Brønsted acid sites, where it becomes proto-
nated to produce an alkylcarbenium ion [11,75]. This intermediate can also be formed by
the direct protonation of the n-alkane, followed by dehydrogenation, but the formation
of the alkylcarbenium ion is more favorable through olefin protonation. Once formed,
the alkylcarbenium ion may undergo skeletal rearrangements to produce mono-, di-, or
tri-branched carbenium ions. At this stage, cracking occurs via β-scission (C–C bond
cleavage), generating branched alkylcarbenium ions (still adsorbed on acid sites) and
smaller olefins [76]. The isomers then desorb from the acid sites and, together with the
olefins produced during cracking, are subsequently hydrogenated on the metallic phase,
producing the corresponding alkanes [76,77].

Catalysts used in the cracking and isomerization of lipid feedstocks should exhibit
moderate acidity in order to promote isomerization while avoiding excessive cracking. The
literature reports several types of acid catalysts, including zeolites in their protonic form (H-
ZSM-5, H-ZSM-22, H-Y, H-β, H-USY), alumina-modified mesoporous silicas (Al-MCM-41,
Al-MCM-48), and silicoaluminophosphates [23,25,78–80].
The overall efficiency of the HEFA process strongly depends on the nature and balance
of the catalytic functions involved. Since deoxygenation, cracking, and isomerization
reactions occur through hydrogenation/dehydrogenation steps and are acid-catalyzed, the
design of suitable heterogeneous catalysts is of great importance. As mentioned, bifunc-

excessive cracking. Moreover, temperature must be carefully controlled, since isomeriza-

higher temperatures (300–400 ◦C) [11]. In this context, extensive research has been devoted
to the development of catalysts with optimized metal-acid balance, stability, and pore
structure, combined with operational conditions. A more detailed understanding of the
heterogeneous catalysts involved in the HEFA process is discussed in the following section.
4. Bifunctional Heterogeneous Catalysts
As previously discussed, the selection of an appropriate catalyst plays a crucial role
in deoxygenation, cracking, and isomerization reactions. Although the HEFA process
generally operates with distinct units for deoxygenation and for cracking/isomerization,
numerous studies in the literature have demonstrated the conversion of lipid feedstocks
into liquid fuels through a single-step process. Therefore, the appropriate choice of the
metal phase and the support is essential.
4.1. Metal Phase
Recent advances in the choice of the metal phase are mainly concentrated around
the use of non-noble transition metals. Regarding the type of feedstock used in the HEFA
process, many aspects must be taken into account, such as the removal of heteroatoms
and hydrogenation reactions. The first critical part for lipid feedstocks is the removal of
oxygen, which approaches the cleavage of C–O and C=O bonds. Metals with elevated
oxophilicity, such as Fe and Mo, are excellent candidates to promote deoxygenation from
triglycerides [24].
Conventional deoxygenation catalysts include Mo, mainly in its sulfide form (MoS2).
Promoters such as Co and Ni are commonly added to enhance the activity of MoS2 by do-
nating electrons to Mo and weakening the metal–sulfur bond [81]. This facilitates hydrogen
dissociation on the metallic phase, thereby improving hydrogenation reactions. Despite
their wide application, metal-sulfide catalysts require the continuous addition of sulfur-
containing compounds to the feedstock to maintain high catalytic activity. This practice
negatively affects both the process and the final product quality, particularly regarding
sulfur content. Furthermore, the water generated as a by-product can significantly suppress
the activity of sulfide catalysts [82].
One of the main drawbacks of using Mo-based catalysts lies in their difficult reduction
behavior. Oxides such as MoO2 and MoO3 are formed when a reducing temperature
between 500 and 600 ◦C is applied [26]. However, Mo-oxides are not stable because they
are prone to oxidation, which lowers their activity over time [83]. Other alternatives
are reported by many authors to improve the stability of Mo species, producing carbide
(Mo2C) or phosphide (MoP), but require elevated temperature under hydrogen to produce
them [84]. Nevertheless, many studies have demonstrated the high activity of MoO2 species
alone and doped with Co or Ni to convert fatty acids and waste cooking oil, demonstrating
the potential to explore oxide species [26,85–87].
Supported Co and Ni catalysts are also frequently employed as active phases. Hy-
drogen molecules dissociate well on Ni metallic particles, making these catalysts highly
efficient for hydrogenation reactions [88]. However, this strong hydrogenation capability
can also promote coke formation, leading to carbon deposition on the active sites and a

exhibit moderate selectivity toward cracking reactions, producing hydrocarbons mainly
in the C10–C17 range from lipid feedstocks [89,90]. In contrast, Co-based catalysts show
excellent selectivity for deoxygenation and hydrogenolysis reactions, and several studies
have reported a higher formation of isomerized products during the conversion of lipids
into liquid hydrocarbons [61,90,91]. On the other hand, Co is somewhat more difficult to
reduce compared to Ni and tends to form metallic aggregates on the catalyst surface, which
decreases metal dispersion and increases the particle size of the metallic phase [92].
Noble metals such as Pt, Pd, and Ir are also highly efficient for hydrogenation reactions.
However, besides their high economic cost, these metals tend to exhibit higher selectivity
toward the DCO and DCO2 pathways [25,72,93].
4.2. Supports
The selection of appropriate support for the reactions involved in the HEFA process
is crucial to ensure good metal dispersion, as well as thermal and mechanical stability.
Moreover, mesoporous materials are particularly suitable for facilitating the cracking of
triglycerides into n-alkanes and iso-alkanes within the catalyst pores. The literature on the
catalytic upgrading of lipid feedstocks reports a wide range of support materials, including
metal oxides, mesoporous silicas, zeolites, and carbon-based materials.
Metal oxides such as SiO2 and γ-Al2O3 are commonly employed in the petrochem-
ical industry as catalyst supports in hydrotreating units, with Co–MoS/Al2O3 being the
most widely used system. In addition to their high mechanical and thermal resistance,
these materials exhibit large specific surface areas (200–500 m2 gcat−1) and predominantly
mesoporous structures [21,94]. However, alumina is known to be metastable in the pres-
ence of water vapor and highly susceptible to structural degradation, undergoing partial
transformation into hydrated boehmite [95].
Silica-based mesoporous materials such as SBA-15, MCM-41, and MCM-48 have
gained significant attention because they combine moderate acidity, high specific sur-
face area, and large pore diameters [96,97].
These materials present several ben-
efits over conventional alumina supports, including higher surface areas (typically
600–1000 m2 gcat−1) [9,98]. Their mesoporous frameworks enable a more effective dis-
persion of active sites both within the pores and on the external surface of the support.
However, the application of such materials in catalytic HDO reactions remains challenging,
as they tend to be structurally unstable under severe hydrothermal conditions. Prolonged
exposure to steam can lead to a gradual loss of surface area and eventual collapse of the
internal pore structure due to partial steam pressure effects [93].
Zeolites are generally microporous crystalline solid acid materials that facilitate the
selective conversion of desired hydrocarbon products (isomers, aromatics, cyclic and
aliphatic compounds). They are composed of corner-sharing TO4 tetrahedra, where T
typically represents Si and Al in their conventional form, giving rise to aluminosilicate
structures. In these frameworks, adjacent SiO4 and AlO4−tetrahedra are interconnected
through shared oxygen atoms [99]. Their tunable acid–base properties also make these
materials highly attractive. In fact, the incorporation of AlO4−tetrahedra into the zeolite
framework introduces a negative charge, which is compensated by protons (H+), giving
rise to Brønsted acid sites (BAS). Moreover, aluminosilicates can also act as Lewis acids
in reactions like Friedel–Crafts alkylation, due to the formation of extra-framework Al3+
species through dealumination [100]. A modernite framework inverted (MFI) zeolite

Among the zeolites employed in the catalytic conversion of lipid-based feedstocks,
the most commonly used are ZSM-5, ZSM-22, zeolite Y, zeolite β, and USY [96,99,101–103].
Despite their widespread use, zeolite-based catalysts present certain drawbacks associated
with their strong acidity, which often leads to excessive cracking and reduced selectivity
toward the desired products [23,80,95,99]. Furthermore, because both the reactants and
products are typically long-chain molecules, zeolites may suffer from diffusion and mass
transfer limitations within their microporous frameworks [61]. To address these issues,
various strategies have been proposed to introduce mesoporosity into zeolite structures,
such as dealumination and desilication treatments, which generate hierarchical frameworks
combining micro- and mesopores [101,103–105]. These structural modifications can en-
hance molecular accessibility and mitigate diffusional constraints. However, highly acidic
zeolite-based supports remain susceptible to rapid deactivation due to the deposition of
carbonaceous species on active sites, ultimately diminishing catalytic performance during
deoxygenation reactions [95,99].
Finally, carbon-based materials are excellent supports, with the possibility to modify
their chemical surface to suit specific properties, such as acidity and porosity. The capacity
of carbon atoms to participate in covalent bonds with other carbon atoms in different
hybridization states (sp, sp2, sp3) or with nonmetallic elements enables them to form a
wide range of structures, from small molecules to long chains [106]. Furthermore, carbon-
based materials have high potential for recyclability and reusability, elevated chemical
stability in acidic or basic media, low corrosion capability, and high thermal stability [107].
Their surface is composed of a variety of organic functional groups, anchoring different
heteroatoms, which contribute to the acid-base character and textural properties [107,108].
For hydroprocessing reactions to convert oils into SAF, some studies have reported
using carbon-based catalysts, often using commercial systems such as Pd/AC and Ru/C.
Other works have investigated the use of activated carbon (AC), CNT, and carbon
nanofibers (CNF) to assess their catalytic performance in the conversion of model com-
pounds, vegetable oils, and WCO [26,29,109,110]. Since the textural properties are a key
parameter for the choice of the supports, the use of AC with extended microporosity is less
suitable, especially if it is applied to a complex feedstock such as WCO. In contrast, CNT
and CNF offer more favorable mesoporous characteristics that facilitate mass transport and
improve the dispersion of active species.
Given their unique structure, high purity, and superior mechanical and chemical
stability, CNTs have emerged as a promising support. The discovery of multi-walled
carbon nanotubes (MWCNT) dates to 1991, when Sumio Iijima identified them in carbon
soot generated via the arc-discharge technique. CNT are based on a hexagonal lattice of sp2
carbon atoms, such as graphene. Single-walled carbon nanotubes (SWCNT) have diameters
of around 0.4–2 nm and are several micrometers long, with an empty internal space, while

different solvents, water, and increase the acid character by the introduction of oxygenated

5. Catalytic Upgrading of Lipid-Based Feedstocks
Given the reactions involved in the conversion of lipid-based feedstocks and the
catalysts required to produce hydrocarbons in the C8–C16 range, this section reviews the
state of the art in the catalytic upgrading of fatty acids, vegetable oils, and WCO. Under-
standing each feedstock individually, starting from model compounds and progressing
to more complex systems, is essential for selecting appropriate reaction conditions and
catalyst formulations.
5.1. Catalytic Conversion of Fatty Acids
Fatty acids are carboxylic acids that constitute triglyceride molecules. Hence, they are
often employed as model compounds to represent the behavior of vegetable oils in catalytic
upgrading studies. Fatty acids can be classified as saturated or unsaturated. Saturated
fatty acids have fully hydrogenated chains and typically remain solid at room temperature,
while unsaturated fatty acids contain one or more double bonds, which lowers their melting
point and generally makes them liquid under ambient conditions [116]. The short-name
nomenclature for fatty acids is typically expressed as the number of carbon atoms in the
chain followed by the number of double bonds. For instance, oleic acid contains 18 carbon
atoms and one double bond, located between carbon 9 and 10, and is therefore designated
as C18:1. In contrast, palmitic acid has a 16-carbon fully saturated chain and is represented
as C16:0 [57].
Due to their well-defined molecular structures, fatty acids allow for a clearer interpreta-
tion of deoxygenation pathways (HDO, DCO, and DCO2), cracking and isomerization, and
help isolate intrinsic catalytic effects without the complexity introduced by multicomponent
systems such as real lipid sources.
