Later, Verma et al. [21] conducted a comprehensive reaction parameters optimization
study over a commercial sulfided Ni-Mo/Al2O3 catalyst using a filtered WCO with 320 and
390 ppm of N and S, respectively. Under optimal conditions, the catalyst achieved 96.6%
WCO conversion and 55.6% selectivity to SAF-range hydrocarbons, with a remarkable
isomer yield of 82.9% and an iso/n ratio of 4.8 (entry 2, Table 5). The jet fraction consisted
mainly of paraffins (43.6%), followed by naphthenes (8.2%) and very low aromatic content
(3.73%). Heteroatom removal was almost complete (99.9% of N, 94.3% of S, and 99.9% of O)
during hydroprocessing.
In addition to these advances, Racha et al. [136] provided further insight into the
catalytic upgrading of WCO through a two-step reaction: hydroprocessing route followed
by isomerization. The authors also tested bimetallic Ni-Mo as a metal phase (Mo in its
sulfide form) but supported on mixed Al2O3-SiO2 oxides. The study focused on how the
Al2O3:SiO2 ratio influences textural properties, metal-support interactions, and the forma-
tion of active MoS2 phases. Among the evaluated formulations, the catalyst supported on
Al2O3-SiO2 with a 70:30 ratio showed the best performance during the HDO step, achieving
86.69% conversion of degummed WCO and 74.8% selectivity to SAF-range hydrocarbons
under relatively mild conditions (entry 3, Table 5).
Recent advances in Ni- and NiCo-based catalysts have demonstrated their strong
potential for converting WCO into SAF. Razak et al. [137] showed that bimetallic Ni-Co
catalysts supported on SBA-15 exhibit enhanced activity when the support is functionalized
with -SH groups, which improves surface area, pore size, and acidity. Metal load was also a
key factor in the performance, since excessive metal loading (10 wt.%) reduced accessibility
to active sites, whereas the Ni-Co/SBA-15-SH catalyst achieved the best performance under
H2-free reaction, producing 80% hydrocarbon yield with 92% SAF selectivity (entry 4,
Table 5). Also under hydrogen-free conditions, Goh et al. [138] developed a Ni-Co/TiO2 cat-
alyst capable of efficiently deoxygenating WCO, achieving 83.13% deoxygenation and 44.5%
selectivity to jet fuel under optimized parameters identified by Box–Behnken methodology
(entry 5, Table 5).
Focusing on monometallic Ni systems, Saviola et al. [139] synthesized Ni supported
on PO4–ZrO2 nanocatalysts, reporting superior acidity, dispersion, and activity compared
to conventional wet impregnation. Their optimal catalyst 1 wt.% Ni/ PO4–ZrO2 yielded
54.7% of SAF (entry 6, Table 5). After five consecutive runs, SAF yield slightly declines to
41.1%. A different approach was explored by Khan et al. [140], who proposed a synergistic
thermocatalytic process coupling catalytic cracking with in situ hydrogen production, using
a Ni/Al2O3 catalyst. Under optimized conditions, the process yielded 80.2% of C8-C16,
with minimal coke (1%), offering a cost-effective dual-output route aligned with circular
economy goals (entry 7, Table 5).
Beyond Ni- and Co-based systems, several studies have explored alternative catalytic
formulations for WCO upgrading. Jamil et al. [141] developed a novel Pt–Fe2O3–ZrO2
catalyst with elevated acidity, porosity, and metal dispersion, enabling 91.3% of total
deoxygenation and the formation of hydrocarbons in the diesel and aviation ranges (entry 8,
Table 5).
Although carbon-based catalysts remain scarce in the hydroprocessing of WCO, the
available studies clearly demonstrate their strong potential. Ferreira et al. [65] evaluated
Ni(Co)-Mo bimetallic catalysts supported on CNT and CNTox for WCO upgrading. Char-
acterization results showed the presence of MoO2 species in all evaluated catalysts, as well
as mixed Co-Mo oxides. Additionally, no significant differences in the total acidity of the
catalysts supported on pristine CNT and oxidatively treated CNT were identified. This
was reflected in the similar performance observed during the screening of the prepared
materials. The Ni–Mo/CNTox catalyst showed no deactivation after four consecutive

Muñoz-Arjona et al. [110] showed that Mo2C supported on CNF (Mo2C/CNF) is
highly effective for HDO, first using stearic acid as a model compound and then applying
the catalyst to real WCO in a trickle-bed reactor. At 350 ◦C, Mo2C/CNF achieved up to
87 mol.% C15–C18 hydrocarbons, predominantly through the direct HDO route, but with
minimal cracking (entry 10, Table 5). Earlier work by Pham et al. [142] also demonstrated the
viability of carbon support, using activated-carbon-supported nickel phosphides (Ni2P/AC)
for atmospheric-pressure HDO of WCO. The authors achieved more than 85% oxygen
removal and produced mainly n-alkanes in the C15–C19 range (entry 11, Table 5). Ribeiro
et al. [143] investigated the conversion of WCO using a physical mixture of Co-Mo/CNT
and H-ZSM-5 in continuous and discontinuous modes. A high content of aromatics
was obtained with these catalysts in the reaction performed in batch (around 75% of
selectivity). On the other hand, the physical mixture provided a higher selectivity in C8-C16
hydrocarbons when performed in a fixed-bed reactor (entry 12, Table 5), showing the
importance of aligning catalyst properties with suitable reaction systems to maximize the
selectivity in the desired products.
Based on the compilation of the main results reported in the literature, there remains
significant room for the development of carbon-based catalysts, particularly those based
on CNT. Important knowledge gaps persist regarding how modifications to the surface
chemistry of these materials, especially the introduction of acidic functional groups through
nitric acid oxidation, can influence product selectivity within the C8–C16 hydrocarbon range.
Furthermore, the literature demonstrates that metal-supported carbon catalysts exhibit
excellent performance in deoxygenation reactions via HDO, with Mo standing out as an
effective active metal phase. Nevertheless, the promotion of cracking and isomerization
reactions remains limited when carbon-based catalysts are employed. Beyond surface
acidity modification, the incorporation of acidic solids (such as zeolites), either through
simple physical mixtures or the formation of hybrid materials, has been scarcely explored,
highlighting the need for further systematic investigations.
6. Catalytic Upgrading of Plastic Waste
In addition to lipid-based feedstocks, other second-generation sources have gained in-
creasing attention as complementary routes to meet the growing demand for SAF. Although
WCO represents an attractive low-cost and low-carbon feedstock, its limited availability is
insufficient to sustain large-scale SAF production [52]. In this context, alternative feedstocks
such as plastic waste emerge as promising candidates due to their high hydrocarbon content,
wide availability, and the urgent need for environmentally responsible disposal pathways.
Global plastic consumption has increased significantly over recent decades, leading to
a substantial rise in plastic waste generation. This growing volume places significant pres-
sure on waste management systems and represents an environmental concern. Since 1950,
approximately 6.3 billion tons of plastic have been produced worldwide, of which only
about 9% has been recycled [144].
From creation to disposal, plastic has detrimental consequences on the ecosystem.
Its end of life causes a number of issues, including the release of toxic gases through
incineration, the formation of microplastics during degradation in the environment or in
landfills, which have an impact on human and animal health, and contribute to landfill
overload due to its prolonged degradation time, even though its production involves the

environmental damage and ensure long-term ecological balance.
There are several classification systems for different types of plastics, which mainly
depend on the stage of the plastic life cycle considered. One of the most widely used
systems is the resin identification code (RIC), designed for both the use and end-of-life
phases and commonly found on consumer packaging. Developed to support recycling
processes, RIC originated the ASTM D7611/D7611M-20. This system defines standardized
symbols and numerical codes for six packaging plastics: polyethylene terephthalate (PET),
high-density polyethylene (HDPE), polyvinyl chloride (PVC), low-density polyethylene
(LDPE), polypropylene (PP), and polystyrene (PS) (all numbered from one to six, while
code seven is reserved for other polymers) [145].
Overall, plastics are distinguished by their chemical composition, with each polymer
consisting of repeating monomer units. Figure 9 illustrates the RIC and the condensed
molecular formula of each polymer. They are composed mainly of C and H atoms and, in
some cases, heteroatoms like O and Cl. Their C–C bonds can be cleaved by hydrogenoly-
sis reactions in the presence of hydrogen pressure, temperature, and a metal-supported
catalyst [13,146]. The produced hydrocarbons can undergo cracking and isomerization
reactions. In this perspective, plastics are attractive feedstocks for jet fuel production due
to their chemical structure and the urgency to give a final disposition to plastic waste.

The production of aviation fuel from plastic residues is not yet consolidated as one of
the technologies defined by the ASTM. However, advances regarding the development of a
new technological route to convert plastics have been recently debated by the ASTM Com-
mittee D02 on Petroleum Products, Liquid Fuels and Lubricants. The proposed standard
route, plastic-to-jet synthetic kerosene with aromatics (PtJ-SKA), admits that the feedstock
is a waste plastic processed oil made from post-consumer waste plastic mainly composed
of LDPE, HDPE, and PP [147]. Consequently, studies involving the conversion of these
feedstocks have been emerging in recent years. The most widely produced industrial
polymers are LDPE and HDPE, and, therefore, they are also the most commonly studied in
the literature [12]. From a process point of view, their conversion could be approached by
applying catalytic systems analogous to those used in the upgrading of lipid-based feed-

Different catalysts have been developed to convert plastic into liquid fuels. Noble
metals such as Ru have a special role in hydrogenolysis reactions and are mostly applied
in polymer reactions. Commercial Ru/C was studied by Jia et al. [148], Rorrer et al. [149],

trends can be observed. Complete conversion is achieved in all cases, confirming its high
activity for hydrogenolysis. However, product distribution depends strongly on reaction
conditions. Higher temperatures and shorter times favor C8–C16 hydrocarbons, whereas
milder conditions require longer reaction times and tend to produce heavier fractions or
lower liquid yields.
