A set of experiments were performed to check the influence of hy­
drocracking temperature, LHSV, pressure and H2/oil ratio on the cata­
lytic hydroprocessing of WCO into biofuels. In addition, a catalyst 
lifetime (TOS) study was also investigated. 
3.1. Influence of hydrocracking temperature on product distribution 
The temperature has been recognized as a critical factor for catalyst 
efficacy and life [25]. The rise in the temperature generates an increase 
in the catalyst activity, which causes the catalyst life to degrade quicker. 
As a result, the influence of reactor temperature on types of reactions 
such as hydrocracking, hydroisomerization and heteroatom removal 
was investigated. The hydroprocessing of WCO is a complicated process 
in which various chemical reactions occur sequentially or simulta­
neously, including hydrogenation/dehydrogenation, deoxygenation, 
hydroisomerization, and hydrocracking. All reactions were carried out 
on WCO hydrocracking at five different temperatures, i.e. 360, 380, 400, 
420, and 440 ◦C. The rest operating parameters were; P = 10 MPa, LHSV 
= 1 h−1, and H2/oil ratio = 2200 NLgas/Lliquid. 
The temperature of the hydrocracking process has a significant 
impact on product yields. The overall liquid product yields were 
calculated using simulated distillation data at each reactor temperature. 
The naphtha, kerosene and diesel yields were determined as the wt% of 
the product having boiling points ranging from 68 to 130 ◦C, 130 to 
260 ◦C and 260 to 331 ◦C, respectively. After the hydrocracking re­
actions, lighter products (gaseous components) and unconverted

The comparison of naphtha, kerosene and diesel product yields 
resulting from the hydrocracking of WCO is shown in Fig. 2(a). Diesel

the lower hydrocracking temperature (360 ◦C). However, as the tem­

The conversion and product selectivities (naphtha, kerosene and

conversion and selectivities were estimated using equations (1) and (2), 
respectively, using the simulated distillation data at each hydrocracking 
temperature. The conversion gradually increased from 98.6 to 99.9 % 
with a rise in the temperature from 360 to 400 ◦C because hydro­
genolysis and hydrocracking activity are endothermic reactions that 
become more favoured with increased temperature. Increment in the 
temperature from 400 to 440 ◦C reduces the conversion from 99.9 to 
96.2 % because the reaction phase shifted primarily to vapour, reducing 
the residence time while maintaining the high hydrogen flow [26]. As a 
result, the conversion rate was reduced due to insufficient reaction time 
[26]. Diesel selectivity was dominant (89.8 %) at the lowest temperature 
(360 ◦C). Further increase in temperature from 360 to 380 ◦C, diesel 
selectivity slightly reduced from 89.8 to 88.3 %, while kerosene selec­
tivity raised from 4.2 to 8.1 %. But when the temperature was increased 
from 380 to 420 ◦C, diesel selectivity was reduced from 88.3 to 6.6 %, 
while the produced kerosene was increased from 8.1 to 55.6 %. More­
over, naphtha selectivity was also increased from 0.3 to 34.1 % when the 
temperature was increased from 360 to 420 ◦C. This was predicted since 
higher temperatures generated more severe cracking, resulting in the 
additional breaking of diesel HCs into kerosene and naphtha (lighter) 
range HCs. As a result, a higher hydrocracking temperature (420 ◦C) 
promotes kerosene selectivity, and lower hydrocracking temperatures 
(360 and 380 ◦C) are suited for diesel production. It should be noted that 
Bezergianni et al. [27] produced kerosene with 20.04 % selectivity from 
catalytic hydrocracking of WCO at 390 ◦C. However, 35.7 % of jet fuel 
selectivity was obtained by Wang et al. [22] using a zeolite-supported 
Ni-Mo catalyst. In another study by Wang et al. [28], bio-jet range 
HCs was synthesized from WCO with 37.3 % selectivity at 380 ◦C. It is 
evident from the research study [22,27,28] that the temperature needed 
for hydrocracking of WCO is lower than the present work. However, 
products obtained in this study have relatively high selectivity towards 
jet range HCs. 
Fig. 2(c) shows the isomer yield and i/n ratio of the products ob­
tained after the hydrocracking of WCO at various reactor temperatures. 
The quantity of isomer was lowest (14.7 %) at 360 ◦C temperature, and 
grew to 69.5 % by raising the temperature from 360 to 400 ◦C and 
reached the maximum of 82.9 % at 420 ◦C. This is understood by the fact 
that higher temperature accelerates the catalyst’s activity, leading to 
increased isomers formation. The amount of isomer declined from 82.9 
to 80.9 % when the reactor temperature increased above 420 ◦C, as a 
shorter reaction time at a higher temperature lowered the efficacy of 
hydroisomerization and hydrocracking. Furthermore, the i/n ratio was 
low (0.17) at 360 ◦C and increased with the temperature. The maximum 
i/n ratio (4.8) was obtained at 420 ◦C. It could be due to the fact 
increasing the reaction temperature to a reasonable level encourages the 
isomerization process. [29]. Hydroisomerization, on the other hand, is a 
weak exothermic process that may be stopped by high temperatures. As 
a result, at 440 ◦C, the number of isomers reduced, and the i/n ratio fell 
to 4.2. Wang et al. [22] produced jet fuel having an i/n ratio of 2.7 using 
core–shell zeolite supported Ni-Mo catalyst at 380 ◦C. Wang et al. 
[26,30] conducted a study in which they employed a two-step process to 
produce jet range HCs with i/n ratio of 0.7. Thus, the i/n ratio in this 
study was significantly higher than the one in [22,26]. It is obvious that 
the catalyst used in this study showed better hydroisomerization selec­
tivity (i/n ratio = 4.8) as compared to the previous studies in a single 
step process using single catalyst, which improve the freezing point of 
the fuel. 
Kerosene range product distribution based on paraffins, naphthenes 
and aromatics (PNA) amounts for five different temperatures are 
compared in Fig. 2(d). When the reaction was carried out between 360 
and 400 ◦C, the contribution of naphthenes and aromatics to the total 
products was negligible, as shown in Fig. 2(d). The product selectivity 
altered as the temperature was raised to 420 ◦C. The formation of par­
affins (43.6 %), naphthenes (8.2 %) and aromatics (3.73 %) was found at 
high temperature (420 ◦C) and pressure (10 MPa). Partially unsaturated 
intermediates may have undergone cyclization proceeded by the dehy­
drogenation process to form highly persistent aromatics at higher tem­
perature (420 ◦C). The product distribution assured the good fluidity 
and oxidative stability of the kerosene range HCs. The aromatic fraction 
of 18.7 % was obtained by Wang et al. [22] from catalytic hydrocracking 
of WCO. Furthermore, Wang et al. [28] produced jet range aromatics 
(7.6 %) using WCO as a feed. Cheng et al. [4] produced high aromatic 
content jet fuel (13.4 %) at 400 ◦C temperature using nickel-based 
mesoporous zeolite catalyst. They have used three types of zeolites 
loaded with nickel to meet jet range specifications. However, in this 
study, a lower amount of aromatics (3.73 %) was produced than 
[4,22,28] in one step method, which meets the jet fuel specifications as 
per the ASTM 1655 standard. A minimal aromatic content is required in 
aviation fuel as they do not burn as efficiently and clearly as alkanes. 
Aromatics have deleterious properties like low heat of combustion and 
poor combustion quality, leading to incomplete combustion and, 
consequently, soot production [31]. Herein, a very less amount of aro­
matics (3.73 %) was formed after the hydrocracking of WCO, which is 
advantageous because less burning of aromatics offers the potential to 
add value to fuel to minimize emission characteristics. A research study 
by Zhang et al. found that the lower aromatic content in jet fuel resulted 
in improved thermal oxidation stability [32]. In another study, Sinha 
et al. [33] eliminated the aromatics present in jet fuel via hydrogenation 
and converted it to cycloalkanes to increase thermal stability and energy 
density as well as minimize soot production. Furthermore, cycloalkane 
formation provides the same potential advantages as aromatics by 
allowing fuels to fulfil density requirements and perhaps giving the seal 
swelling capacity given by aromatics today. The jet fuel range HCs, 
which have cycloalkanes and isoalkanes, burns significantly cleaner and 
might boost energy density and specific energy, also fulfilling flash 
point, freezing point and O-ring swelling requirements [34]. 
Besides hydrocracking and isomerization reactions, heteroatom (ni­

occurs during hydroprocessing reactions. Despite the fact that a signif­

modulate catalyst activity, the resulting kerosene HCs had just content 
of sulfur. The absolute values of N, S and O for feed and hydrocracking 
products at five different temperatures are given in Table 2. Fig. 2(e) 
compares the removal of heteroatoms as a percentage of their content in 
the feed at five temperatures during hydrocracking processes. The figure 
shows that nitrogen was the most successfully eliminated of the three 
elements, with a removal rate of over 98.3 % in all cases. The highest 
removal of nitrogen (99.8 %) was found at 420 ◦C. Sulfur was also 
successfully removed by over 91.5 % for all temperatures. However, the 
final product included a minimal quantity of sulfur (27–33 ppm), as seen 
in Table 2. Removal of sulfur gradually increased with increasing tem­
perature. In particular, the sulfur removal rate increased from 91.5 % at 
380 ◦C to 93 % at 440 ◦C. Furthermore, eliminating oxygen from the 
feed was the most difficult task. The amount of oxygen in the product 
was derived indirectly after the amount of C, H, N, and S were measured 
in the product, rather than by direct analytical testing. The lowest (97.5

3.2. Influence of LHSV on product distribution 
LHSV, a significant operational factor in continuous hydroprocessing 
processes for controlling catalyst efficacy and also catalyst life expec­
tancy, is defined as the residence time of the feed molecules on the 
catalyst surface while passing through the reactor. The basic premise of 
this investigation is the influence of LHSV on hydrocracking and 
hydroisomerization. The hydroprocessing of WCO was investigated at 
five various LHSVs (0.5, 1, 2, 3, and 5 h−1) and at constant reaction 
parameters; T = 420 ◦C, P = 10 MPa, and H2/oil = 2200 NLgas/Lliquid. 
The conversion and the selectivities of naphtha, kerosene and diesel 
at five different LHSVs are depicted in Fig. 3(a). It is evident from figure 
that as LHSV increased, conversion decreased from 99.9 (LHSV = 0.5 
h−1) to 99.2 % (LHSV = 5 h−1). The drop in the conversion with greater 
LHSV was expected since higher LHSVs result in a shorter reaction time 
between catalyst and feed. Nevertheless, the conversion was above 96.6 
% at all LHSVs. On the other hand, regarding the individual product 
selectivities, the maximum kerosene selectivity (55.6 %) was observed 
at 1 h−1. When the LHSV increased from 1 to 5 h−1, a drastic drop in 
kerosene selectivity was observed from 55.6 to 21.9 %. In the meantime, 
naphtha selectivity also decreased from 34.1 (1 h−1) to 13.6 % (5 h−1), 
while diesel selectivity increased from 6.6 to 57.4 %. Therefore, kero­
sene and naphtha selectivities were not promoted by LHSV, while LHSV 
favoured diesel selectivity. It is possible that rising LHSV decreases the 
reaction time, resulting in less cracking and, as a result, fewer lighter 
molecules being produced (kerosene and naphtha). 
Isomer yield and i/n ratio at different LHSVs are compared in Fig. 3 
(b). It is clear from the figure that low isomer yield (64.6 %) and i/n ratio 
(1.82) were obtained at the lowest LHSV (0.5 h−1) as the product could 
not move out promptly and hampered the consecutive cracking and 
isomerization reactions on the catalyst surface. An increase in LHSV to 1 
h−1 led to the rise in isomer yield and i/n ratio to 82.9 % and 4.8, 
respectively, showing that reactant and catalysts have adequate contact 
time for isomerization reactions. Further increase in the LHSV to 2 h−1, a 
sharp reduction in isomer yield (59.6 %) and i/n ratio (1.47) was 
observed. It is possible that the comparatively high input flow rate of 
feed caused insufficient hydroisomerization reaction time, resulting in a 
dramatic fall in isomer yields and i/n ratio. Moreover, a rise in LHSV to 3 
and 5 h−1, 57.2 % and 55.7 % of isomer yield and 1.34 and 1.25 of i/n 
ratio was achieved, which is also in decreasing trend. An increase in the 
LHSV reduced the reaction time between the catalysts and feed, which 
reduced the hydroisomerization reaction rate and, hence, was respon­
sible for reducing the yield of isomers and the i/n ratio. In a recent study, 
Wang et al. [26] produced jet range HCs with a maximum i/n ratio value 
of nearly 1.2 at 1 h−1 LHSV in a two-stage process. It is clear that 
products obtained in the current study showed better hydro­
isomerization selectivity (4.8 i/n ratio at 1 h−1) in a single stage process 
as compared to earlier report [26]. 
The corresponding product distribution (PNA) of kerosene range HCs 
is compared for five different LHSVs in Fig. 3(c). The figure revealed a 
significant contribution of paraffins and cycloalkanes followed by aro­
matics for LHSVs below 2 h−1. Specifically, increasing the LHSV from 
0.5 to 1 h−1, the amount of paraffins, naphthenes and aromatics 
increased from 35.8 to 43.6 %, 6.8 to 8.2 %, and 1.7 to 3.7 %, respec­
tively. However, the contribution of naphthenes and aromatics was 
insignificant for LHSVs ranging from 3 to 5 h−1 as higher LHSVs suggest 
a shorter reaction time of the feed and catalyst within the reactor, 
resulting in a lower possibility of the production of cycloalkanes and 
aromatics. 
Sulfur and nitrogen were efficiently eliminated by over 91.7 and 
96.9 %, respectively for all cases, as shown in Fig. 3(d). Specifically,

3.3. Influence of pressure on product distribution 
Pressure is an essential factor because it influences the reactions such 
as hydrogenation, hydrocracking, and hydroisomerization. In this sec­
tion, four experiments were carried out at 8, 9, 10, and 11 MPa. The 
other additional reaction conditions were; T = 420 ◦C, LHSV = 1 h−1, 
and H2/oil = 2200 NLgas/Lliquid. 
The conversion and product selectivities at different pressures are 
depicted in Fig. 4(a). It is evident from the figure that a nearly equal 
percentage of kerosene selectivity (38 %) was observed at 8 and 9 MPa 
pressure. When the pressure was raised from 9 to 10 MPa, the kerosene 
selectivity increased to 55.6 %. Moreover, the contribution of kerosene 
was significant at all pressures. Remarkably, kerosene exhibited the 
maximum selectivity (55.6 %) at 10 MPa pressure, while diesel pro­
duction decreased with pressure due to more intense cracking. Mainly, 
the diesel selectivity decreased from 32.8 to 5.2 % as the pressure 
increased from 8 to 11 MPa, indicating more hydrocracking reactions at 
higher pressure. It is worth noting that kerosene emerged as one of the 
leading biofuels after the hydrocracking of WCO at all pressures. 
Fig. 4(b) demonstrates the isomer yield and i/n ratio at four different 
hydrotreatment pressures. Isomer yield of 72.4 % and an i/n ratio of 2.6 
was observed at the lowest pressure (8 MPa). Increase in the pressure 
from 8 to 9 MPa, isomer yield and i/n ratio increased to 75 % and 3, 
respectively. The maximum isomer yield (82.9 %) and i/n ratio (4.8) 
were observed when the pressure was raised to 10 MPa. It could be due 
to the fact that high pressure was responsible for hydroisomerization, 
which led to a high i/n ratio. 
The product distribution for kerosene range HCs based on PNA 
selectivity at four different pressures was compared in Fig. 4(c). It is 
evident from the figure that 29.7 % of paraffins followed by 4 % of 
naphthenes and 4.9 % of aromatic HC selectivity, were observed at the 
lowest hydrotreatment pressure (8 MPa). Highest aromatics found at the 
same pressure as low pressure favours the formation of aromatics as it 
promotes cycloalkane dehydrogenation. Rise in the pressure from 8 to 
10 MPa, the amount of paraffins (43.6 %) and naphthenes (8.2 %) 
increased, while aromatics (3.7 %) decreased. 
The concentration of nitrogen, sulfur and oxygen reduced from the 
feed during the hydroprocessing was used to determine the percentage 
of heteroatom removal. 
Nitrogen was most efficiently removed by over 99.6 % at all pres­
sures, as shown in Fig. 4(d). Sulfur was also successfully eliminated in 
almost 88.9 % of all conditions. Furthermore, the most challenging 
molecule to eliminate was oxygen, which was effectively eliminated in 
all operating conditions by over 98.5 %. In particular, maximum oxygen 
was removed (99.8 %) at 10 MPa pressure. It is evident from the figure 
that all the pressures favour the removal of all the heteroatoms from the 
final products.

3.4. Influence of H2/oil ratio on product distribution 
An essential operational factor in hydroprocessing is the H2/oil ratio 
since it is believed to have the greatest influence on the efficiency of 
hydrocracking and hydrogenation reactions. The H2/oil ratio, in this 
work, refers to the hydrogen supply over WCO feed. In this section, four 
experiments were conducted at four H2/oil ratios (1800 to 2400 NLgas/ 
Lliquid). The rest parameters were; T = 420 ◦C, P = 10 MPa, LHSV =
1hr−1. 
The conversion and product selectivity at four different H2/oil ratios 
were compared in Fig. 5(a). The results indicated that the maximum 
WCO conversion (99.9 %) was observed at a lower H2/oil ratio (1800 
and 2000 NLgas/Lliquid). However, when the H2/oil ratio increased, WCO 
conversion efficiency declined, which was predicted since unwanted 
cracking and hydrogenation reactions may occur along with favourable 
hydroprocessing reactions. Moreover, kerosene selectivity was favoured 
by increasing the H2/oil ratio, as shown in Fig. 5(a). Kerosene selectivity 
risen from 40.6 to 55.6 %, while diesel selectivity decreased from 25 to 
6.6 % when the H2/oil ratio increased from 2000 to 2200 NLgas/Lliquid. It 
might be due to the fact that heavy and diesel range HCs convert into 
lower range HCs, particularly in kerosene and naphtha range HCs. 
Further, an increase in the H2/oil ratio to 2400 NLgas/Lliquid did not 
favour kerosene range formation. High hydrogen pressure and rise in 
H2/oil ratio may cause a diffusion constraint of reactants and in­
termediates. It is important to note that more than 40 % kerosene 
selectivity was observed at all H2/oil ratios, indicating that 40 % of the 
WCO has transformed into kerosene at all H2/oil ratios. 
Isomer yield and i/n ratio at four H2/oil ratios were compared in 
Fig. 5(b). The results showed that increasing the H2/oil ratio enhanced 
isomer yield and i/n ratio, and the highest isomer yield (82.9 %) was 
found at 2200 NLgas/Lliquid H2/oil ratio. The high H2/oil ratio enhanced 
the reaction rate of isomerization reactions, which improved the isomer 
production. Further increase in H2/oil ratio from 2200 to 2400 NLgas/ 
Lliquid lowered the residence time, resulting in an inefficient reaction due 
to which the reactants were unable to change into isomers. It is worth 
noting that H2 is an energy source; therefore, it is aimed at an operating 
state that uses the least amount of hydrogen possible. 
Product distribution based on PNA selectivity of kerosene range HCs 
at four different H2/oil ratios was compared in Fig. 5(c). It was evident 
from the figure that 28.5 % of paraffins, 1.5 % of naphthenes, and 4.8 % 
of aromatics (maximum) were found at the lowest H2/oil ratio (1800 
NLgas/Lliquid). Low H2/oil ratios promote the dehydrogenation of alkanes 
and cycloalkanes, leading to aromatics formation. Further increase in 
the H2/oil ratio to 2200 NLgas/Lliquid decreased the aromatics to 3.7 % 
while increasing the formation of paraffins and naphthenes to 43.6 and 
8.29 %, respectively. 
Fig. 5(d) compares the removal percentage of heteroatoms as a 
percent of their content in the feed at four H2/oil ratios. Table 2 shows 
the amount of heteroatoms in the finished product. Nitrogen was the 
most successful element removed with over 99.5 %, whereas sulfur was 
similarly eliminated with over 91 %, as illustrated in Fig. 5(d). 
Furthermore, oxygen was also successfully removed by over 98.3 % at 
all H2/oil ratios. Specifically, 99.1 % of oxygen was removed at the 
lowest H2/oil ratio (1800 NLgas/Lliquid). An increase in the H2/oil to 
2000 and 2200 NLgas/Lliquid showed an increase in the oxygen removal 
rate to 99.6 and 99.8 %, respectively. Further rise in H2/oil ratio showed 
a decrease in the oxygen removal rate to 98.3 %.

The process involves two stages for the production of jet fuel. In the first 
stage, hydroprocessing of WCO has been conducted using two catalysts 
to achieve the alkanes ranging from C15 to C18, which are further sent 
to another reactor to match the jet fuel standards. In the second stage, 
hydrocracking and hydroisomerization of the mixture of n-alkanes 
(obtained in the first stage) took place to optimize reaction conditions 
and jet fuel standard specifications. This process requires multiple cat­
alysts and reactors to achieve the maximum biojet yield. In another 
study, Cheng et al. [4] used WCO as a feedstock and produced jet fuel 
with 53 % alkane selectivity and 13.4 % aromatic HC selectivity. The 
produced fuel has a higher aromatic content resulting in higher partic­

from WCO by Wang et al. [22] in a continuous flow fixed bed reactor, 
and 39.7 % of biojet yield was obtained with an i/n ratio of 2.7. In this 
work, all processes like hydrogenation, cracking, and isomerization 
were performed concurrently in a single reactor and using a catalyst 
with strong hydrogenation/dehydrogenation ability along with desir­
able cracking function. Therefore, we have selectively produced avia­
tion kerosene (55.6 % selectivity) with 8.2 % of naphthenes and 3.7 % of 
aromatics in a single step process using a low-cost feedstock (WCO). One 
of the potential reasons for the generation of naphthenes and aromatics 
in the current method might be the high exothermicity seen during these 
concurrent reactions in a single reactor [36].

reactor to minimize organic molecules and metal deposition over the

cess economics. 
3.7. Plausible hydrocracking mechanism for converting WCO to kerosene 
range HCs 
We proposed a plausible mechanism for converting WCO to kerosene 
range HCs via the catalytic hydroprocessing route. Hydroprocessing of 
glycerides involves the hydrogenation of double bonds (unsaturation) 
within the side chains [37] and the removal of oxygen at the metal 
sulfide site [38]. The fatty acids present in the feed containing double 
bonds in the chain included palmitoleic acid (C16:1), oleic acid (C18:1) 
and linoleic acid (C18:2), as shown in Table 1. The double bonds present 
in the carbon chain of triglycerides are first saturated via the hydroge­
nation process in the presence of the catalyst at 420 ◦C and 10 MPa 
pressure. The propane backbone is then removed from the hydrogenated 
glycerides, which form free fatty acids (FFA). The produced FFAs are 
then converted into straight chain alkanes through the deoxygenation 
process, which includes hydrodeoxygenation (HDO), decarbonylation 
(DCO), and decarboxylation (DCO2) reactions [37], leading to the for­
mation of the octadecane compound [39]. A certain level of hydro­
cracking/isomerization is accompanied by deoxygenation at high 
temperatures and pressure to form naphtha, kerosene and diesel HC 
range compounds [40–43]. Alkanes underwent dehydrogenation, which 
resulted in the formation of alkene molecules. These alkene compounds 
cyclized through the carbenium ion and formed the tertiary cyclic car­
bocation. These cyclic carbocations are further converted into two types 
of compounds; one is substituted naphthenes, and another one is 
substituted aromatics [40,43].
