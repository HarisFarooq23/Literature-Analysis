The usage of petroleum-based jet fuels pollutes the environment as 
petroleum fuels are the major producer of greenhouse gases (GHG). 
Apart from these pollutants, petroleum-based fuels are the substantial 
generator of NOx, SOx, CO, particulate matter, and volatile organic 
compounds [1]. Biomass-derived aviation fuel has long been seen as a 
renewable energy choice as it minimizes the usage of arable land, GHG 
radiation and reliance on fossil fuels. Renewable jet fuel has been taken 
into consideration as a potential renewable opportunity and long-term 
solution to relieve the approaching troubles in the aviation industry. 
From 2008 to 2025, aviation fuel global consumption is predicted to rise 
by nearly 38 %, with an annual enhancement rate of 1.9 % [2]. Biomass- 
derived aviation fuel can substitute about 30 % of total aviation fuel [3]. 
Furthermore, when compared to petroleum-based jet fuel, bio-jet 
fuel may lessen GHG emissions by 65–85 % [4]. For manufacturing 
biomass-derived aviation fuel, many paths have been explored, 
including catalytic sugar-to-jet process [5], gasification to Ficher 
Tropsch process [6], alcohol-to-jet process [7] and hydroprocessed 
renewable jet (HRJ) [8]. One of the most important methods for turning 
non-edible oil-based feeds into aviation fuel is HRJ. Several conversion 
methods have been developed and tested on a commercial or pre- 
commercial scale in recent years. The HRJ method has been regarded 
as the sole commercially feasible technique, and several test flights using 
HRJ fuel have been developed [9]. In the hydroprocessing process, tri­
glycerides present in the feedstock transform into straight-chain alkanes

Apart from the necessity of creating and utilizing biofuels, there are 
various challenges with the current manufacturing procedures. The 
feedstock is an important and crucial part of the procedure. WCO is the 
oil remainder left over after cooking that has deposited free fatty acids 
allowing it a potentially effective and feasible biofuel feedstock [10]. 
WCO is two to three times less expensive than pure vegetable oils, 
significantly lowering the cost of manufacturing biofuels [11]. The 
conversion of WCO to biofuels also reduces the severe environmental 
effects of dumping spent oils into drainage systems [12]. Furthermore, 
the hydroconversion of triglycerides is a complex process (saturation of 
double bonds, heteroatom removal, cracking, and isomerization) for 
which bi-functional catalysts are essential. Frequently, NiMo sulfide 
phase is employed for the hydrocracking process, due to its high hy­
drogenation and hydrodesulfurization activity [13–15]. The support 
must have a high surface area and large pore size to allow the bulk 
molecules to access the active sites located inside the pores easily. The 
silica and alumina are frequently utilized supports in this kind of process 
due to their low cost, excellent thermal and hydrothermal stability. 
Sulfided hydrocracking catalysts are obviously suitable options for 
deoxygenating triglycerides in non-edible oils to biofuels. However, 
relatively little is known about their activity and selectivity towards jet 
range hydrocarbons from WCO. 
Bezergianni et al. have carried out numerous experiments for cata­
lytic hydroprocessing of WCO into high-quality fuels through one 
(hydrotreating) and two-step (hydrotreating + isomerization) methods 
[16]. Hydroprocessing of WCO has been studied utilizing three catalysts; 
a hydrotreating, a mild and a severe hydrocracking catalyst from tem­
perature 330 to 390 ◦C and 8.27 to 13.79 MPa pressure under fixed 
LHSV (1 h−1) [17]. The results indicate that hydrotreating catalyst 
exhibited the highest conversion (83 %), and diesel yields were over 94 
% in all cases. The temperature was identified as the most important 
factor in transforming WCO into biofuels [18]. The influence of hydro­
treating pressure, LHSV and H2/oil ratio on biofuel generation was also 
investigated [19]. The reaction route for synthesizing WCO to biojet fuel 
has been suggested by Li et al. The products contained 53 % jet range 
alkanes and 13.4 % aromatic HCs [4]. Cheng et al. used soybean oil as a 
feedstock and converted it into jet fuel with 40.3 % alkanes and 23.8 % 
aromatics HCs [20]. Another study by Liu et al. in which they have 
developed HRJ fuel by using castor oil, yielding 91.6 wt% aviation range 
alkanes and an i/n ratio of 4.4–7.2 [21]. Hydroconversion of WCO into 
biofuels was reported with 39.7 % jet fuel selectivity and 18.7 % jet 
range aromatics [22]. Nevertheless, a minimum concentration of aro­
matics must be present in the HRJ fuel in order to reduce soot (carbon 
particles) production from the airplane engine [23]. There have been 
few kinds of research conducted to evaluate the impact of catalyst and 
operating parameters on product distribution in the final products. 
Efficient catalysts with longer lifetime and stability towards HRJ pro­
duction from hydroprocessing of WCO in a single-step process using a 
single catalyst remain under-explored and need to be studied further to 
produce HRJ fuel with significant concentrations of paraffins, naph­
thenes and minimum amount of aromatics as well as high isomers for­
mation to improve the jet fuel quality. 
In light of this, the present study focuses on the hydroprocessing of 
WCO in a fixed-bed down flow reactor over Ni-Mo/silica-alumina 
catalyst to look into its suitability to produce bio-aviation fuel. The 
products with high concentrations of paraffins, cycloparaffins, low ar­
omatics and high isomerization (i/n ratio) are the primary target to meet 
the jet fuel specifications. Such information is necessary for hydro­
cracking catalysts to be further optimized in order to become effective 
deoxygenation and selective cracking catalysts with high stability and a 
longer lifetime. No reports are available for the hydrocracking process to 
produce Jet A-1 aviation fuel from inedible WCO in a single-step process 
with high isomerization, minimum aromatics, and longer lifetime and 
stability of the catalyst, which makes this work new and important. 
2. Methodology 
2.1. Materials 
The feedstock utilized in this study was 100 % WCO and collected 
from local hotels, households and restaurants of Dehradun, India, after 
being widely used for frying. The WCO was filtered through a standard 
sieve before being introduced to the experimental area to eliminate any 
food material that remained in the oil after cooking/frying. Table 1 lists 
the typical chemical properties of WCO. For this study, a commercial Ni- 
Mo/silica-alumina catalyst was employed for the catalytic hydro­
processing of WCO in all the experiments. The catalyst is presulfided, as 
described in Section 2.3. 
2.2. Experimental setup 
In this study, all hydrocracking experimental tests for converting 
WCO to kerosene range HCs were carried out in a fixed bed down flow 
microreactor with a commercial hydrocracking catalyst. This type of 
microreactor can be employed for catalytic hydroprocessing reactions at 
high temperatures and pressures. The microreactor can be characterized 
into four sections: hydrogen section, feed insertion section, reactor 
section and product collection/separation section, as depicted in Fig. 1. 
A back pressure regulator (BPR, Tescom) and a mass flow controller 
(MFC, Brooks) were used to properly manage the hydrogen pressure and 
gas flow of the reactor. The reactor was heated by an electric furnace 
(Serial no 10-5887-1-2). The interior and exterior temperatures of the 
reactor were monitored with Type K thermocouples calibrated to 1 K 
accuracy and controlled with a temperature controller. A high-pressure 
liquid metering pump (Eldex) was used in the system to regulate the 
liquid flow of the feed properly. Uniform contact of the feedstock with 
catalyst and hydrogen causes a hydrotreating/hydrocracking process at 
high temperature and hydrogen pressure in the fixed bed reactor. After 
direct contact of feedstock and catalyst within the reactor, the product 
reaches a high-pressure separator (HPS) in the form of a mixed 
gas–liquid phase. HPS was used to remove the gaseous components, 
including hydrogen and some other lighter gases, such as propane, 
carbon dioxide, and carbon mono oxide, from the hydrocracked

2.3. Experimental procedure 
To maintain the required bed length of the catalyst and improve the 
thermal resistance of the reaction, a non-sulphided catalyst (4 g or 5.5 
ml) was blended with SiC (5.5 ml) and introduced into the microreactor 
(45.5 cm length). The catalyst was put in the centre of the reactor to 
ensure adequate heating and flow stability. The catalyst utilized in this 
study is presulfided using 2.5 wt% of Dimethyl Disulphide (DMDS) in 
straight run gas oil (SRGO) to retain the catalytic activity as indicated by 
the catalyst supplier. Before carrying out the experiments, the catalyst 
was dried in the reactor at 175 ◦C for 5 h. Then after, SRGO was used to 
sulfide the catalyst at 250 ◦C, 300 ◦C, 320 ◦C, and 350 ◦C for 5, 5, 6 and 2 
h, respectively. 
The sulfiding agent DMDS (0.025 wt%) was utilized in combination 
with feed to limit the sulfur reduction from the catalyst. Firstly, a 
waiting period of 12 h (first period) was set out for the reaction process 
to stabilize before collecting the final products (second period). A GC 
analysis of the products collected at both periods was performed to 
confirm the persistent catalytic activity and ensure that the catalyst was 
not deactivated. Products were categorized into three groups based on 
their carbon number; <C9 carbon range as naphtha, C9 - C15 as kero­
sene, and C16 - C18 as diesel. Individual products were identified using 
GC standards. 
A detailed study was used to calculate the conversion based on the 
mole balance of total liquid products. The triglyceride concentration 
converted to any form of intermediates or HCs is known as triglyceride 
conversion. The mole balance was 96–99 % precise for all operating

2.4. Product analysis 
The obtained products after the hydrocracking reactions were 
distilled into naphtha, kerosene and diesel range HCs using a 9600 High- 
Efficiency Distillation Unit (B/R instruments, USA). The simulated 
distillation curve was determined via Varian 3800-GC according to the 
ASTM-2887-D86 method. Rudolph research analytical equipment 
(DDM-2911) was employed for measuring the density of feed and final 
liquid products. A residual gas analyzer (RGA) from Agilent 7890A with 
two TCD detectors was used to analyze the gases. Elementar Micro-cube 
CHNS analyzer was employed for determining C, H, N and S percentages 
in the feedstock and products. The liquid products and unconverted 
WCO obtained after the hydrocracking reactions (isomerization, deox­
ygenation, cracking) were analyzed after separating the water phase by 
gas chromatography (GC) analysis (Agilent Technologies GC (7890B, 
G3440B)) fitted with a flame ionization detector (FID) and thermal 
conductivity detector (TCD). Vf-5 ms column (30 m × 0.25 mm, 0.25 
um) was used for quantification. At first, the column temperature was 
maintained at 40 ◦C with a holding period of 0.5 min; after that 
increased to 70 ◦C, 150 ◦C, 200 ◦C, 350 ◦C with the rate of 3 ◦C, 5 ◦C, 
15 ◦C, 20 ◦C per min, and lastly 370 ◦C with the rate of 30 ◦C per min 
having 12 min holding time. Errors in the experiments were also iden­
tified and accounted for in the final results. All the ASTM methods were 
applied in accordance with the “Standard Specification for Aviation 
Turbine Fuel containing Synthesized Hydrocarbons” [24], and the 
aviation kerosene was tested particularly for ASTM D7566.
